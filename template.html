About this collection

Many of the ideas people bring to physics are not gaps in their knowledge but well-built intuitions that work most of the time. They grow out of everyday experience, everyday language reinforces them, and they survive teaching remarkably well: a student can learn the correct rule, pass a test on it, and still reason with the old idea when a question looks unfamiliar. Plenty of adults carry them too, science teachers included.

Being told the right answer rarely shifts these ideas. What works better is to bring the old idea into the open first: commit to a prediction, discover it was wrong, then work out why. Every entry here is built that way. It opens with a question, and only after you answer does it name the misconception, explain why it is tempting, and offer a better picture.

Each entry also suggests ways to tackle the idea in class and places to spot it in everyday life, and ends with a Going deeper section for specialists and for students looking for an extension.

In class: show a question and have everyone commit to an answer, perhaps fingers up for a to d. Let pairs try to convince each other, vote again, then reveal. Printing: print an entry before answering for a question sheet, or after answering for the full entry.
