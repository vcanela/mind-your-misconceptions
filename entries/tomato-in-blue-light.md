## Question

In a dark room, a ripe red tomato is lit only by pure blue light.

What colour does the tomato look?

## Option

Red: the tomato is red, whatever light falls on it.

> In white light the tomato looks red because it reflects the red part of the light and absorbs most of the rest. Pure blue light contains no red, so there is no red for the tomato to reflect. The tomato doesn't make red light; it can only send back red that is already in the light falling on it.

## Option

Purple: some of the blue light mixes with the tomato's red.

> Red and blue light arriving at your eye together do make a purplish colour. But here only blue light reaches the tomato, and the tomato absorbs most of it. There is no red light anywhere in the room to mix.

## Option

Blue: it reflects most of the blue light falling on it.

> A white object, or a blue one, would look blue in this light. A red tomato absorbs most of the blue light falling on it, so it sends back very little of anything.

## Option (correct)

Almost black: it absorbs most of the blue light.

> Right. The tomato's skin absorbs most of the blue light, and there is no red light for it to reflect, so very little light comes back from it. It looks very dark, perhaps with a faint bluish shine where its glossy surface reflects the light directly.

## Why it's tempting

We almost always see the world in daylight or in white-ish artificial light, so the role of the light is invisible. Day after day the tomato looks red, and it seems as natural to call redness a property of the tomato as its shape or its mass.

Language encourages it: we say the tomato *is* red, not that it *looks* red in white light.

Our brains encourage it too. The visual system adjusts for the colour of the lighting, so that a tomato looks much the same red in the warm light of a kitchen as in the bluish light of an overcast day. This colour constancy is useful, but it hides how much the light we see depends on the light that falls.

And the purple answer probably comes from mixing paints and mixing lights, two different processes that both get called "mixing colours".

## A better picture

**White light is a mixture.** Sunlight and white lamps contain light of a whole range of wavelengths, which we see as the colours of the rainbow when they are separated.

**Surfaces absorb and reflect.** When light falls on an object, the surface absorbs some wavelengths and reflects or scatters others. The colour we see depends on the mixture of light that reaches our eyes from the object (and, as we'll see, on how the brain interprets it).

**The tomato.** A ripe tomato's red pigment, lycopene, strongly absorbs blue and green light. In white light, mostly red light comes back, so the tomato looks red. In pure blue light there is almost nothing it can send back, so it looks dark.

**So what does "red" mean?** Saying a tomato is red is shorthand for saying that it looks red in white light. What the tomato actually has is a pattern of absorbing and reflecting: it reflects red well and blue poorly. Change the light, and the colour we see changes with it.

**Two kinds of mixing.** Mixing lights adds wavelengths: red and green light together look yellow, and red, green and blue together look white. Mixing paints subtracts them: each pigment absorbs part of the light, so the mixture absorbs more and looks darker.

## In the classroom

- **Do it.** In a darkened room, light fruit, vegetables and coloured paper with a coloured torch, a coloured LED, or a torch covered with a coloured filter. Have students predict each colour first. Blue LEDs work best: a white torch behind blue cellophane often lets through some deep red, and the tomato then looks dark red or purple rather than black.
- **Secret messages.** Write a message in red on white paper and look at it through a red filter. The message almost disappears: both the red ink and the white paper send back red light, so through the filter they look nearly the same.
- **Mixing lights.** Shine red, green and blue LED torches onto a white wall in a darkened room and overlap them. Yellow from red and green surprises most students.
- **Language.** Try "looks red in white light" now and then, to keep the light in the picture.
- **Where it fits.** Light and colour in Years 7 to 10; spectra and photons later on.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Sodium street lights.** Low-pressure sodium lamps, once common on roads and in car parks, give out almost pure yellow-orange light. Under them, red, blue and green cars all look grey, brown or black, and finding your own car can be a challenge.
- **The dress.** In 2015, a photograph of a striped dress divided the internet: some people saw it as blue and black, others as white and gold. The dress was in fact blue and black. The best explanation is that different brains made different assumptions about the colour of the light falling on it.
- **Shopping for clothes.** A shirt that looks one colour in a shop can look quite different outdoors, because the shop's lighting and daylight contain different mixtures of wavelengths.

## Going deeper

**Colour is made in the brain.** Our eyes have three kinds of colour-sensitive cells (cones), sensitive to overlapping ranges of wavelength. The colour we perceive comes from comparing their responses. As a result, pure yellow light and a suitable mixture of red and green light look identical to us, although physically they are completely different. Screens rely on this: they make a wide range of colours from just red, green and blue light, though not quite every colour we can see.

**Colours with no wavelength.** Magenta doesn't appear in the rainbow at all. There is no single wavelength of light that looks magenta: the brain constructs it from a mixture of red and blue light, with little green.

**Colour without pigment.** Not all colour comes from absorbing light. The blue of a morpho butterfly's wings and the shimmer of a soap bubble come from interference between light waves reflected from the two surfaces of a thin film (the bubble) or from stacks of microscopic layers (the butterfly's wing scales). The blue of a clear sky comes from scattering by air molecules, which scatter blue light more strongly than red.
