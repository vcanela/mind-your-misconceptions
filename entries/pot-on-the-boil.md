## Question

At sea level, a pot of water is boiling steadily on a stove, with its lid off. You turn the burner up to full.

What happens to the temperature of the water?

## Option

It drops slightly, because faster boiling carries energy away faster.

> Boiling faster does carry energy away faster, but the burner is now supplying it faster too. The two balance at the boiling point, so the temperature stays at about 100 °C; only the rate of boiling changes.

## Option

It rises a few degrees, to a new, higher steady temperature.

> A natural expectation: more energy in should mean at least somewhat hotter. But once water is boiling steadily, it stays at its boiling point. Only a thin layer right against the hot base gets a few degrees hotter, and the extra energy goes into making steam faster.

## Option (correct)

It stays at about 100 °C.

> Right. Once water is boiling, extra energy goes into turning more water into steam, not into raising its temperature. With the burner turned up, the water boils away faster, but its temperature stays at the boiling point.

## Why it's tempting

We boil water by heating it, so it is natural to think of boiling as the hottest stage of heating, the point at which water is "really hot". The language helps: "boiling hot" is the everyday phrase for extreme heat.

A vigorous boil also looks more energetic than a gentle one. More bubbles, more noise and more steam all suggest higher temperature.

And in most of everyday experience, adding energy does raise the temperature. The idea that energy can flow in while the temperature stays put is genuinely surprising.

## A better picture

**Energy goes into separating the molecules.** In liquid water, molecules attract each other. To turn water into steam, they have to be pulled apart, and that takes energy: the latent heat of vaporisation, about 2.26 million joules for every kilogram. That is nearly seven times as much energy as it takes to heat the same kilogram of water from 20 °C to 100 °C.

**Boiling carries energy away.** Only molecules with enough energy to break free can escape as steam. Turning water into steam takes energy, and the steam carries it away. A pot keeps boiling only because the burner keeps replacing the energy the steam carries off. Turn the heat off, and the boiling soon dies away: almost at once on a gas flame, more slowly on an electric hob, whose hot plate keeps supplying energy for a while.

**The boiling point depends on pressure.** At sea level, water boils at about 100 °C. Where the air pressure is lower, high in the mountains, water boils at a lower temperature; in a pressure cooker, where the pressure is higher, it boils at a higher one.

**The reverse processes give energy out.** When steam condenses, or water freezes, the molecules come together and give out energy to their surroundings. Boiling and melting take energy in; condensing and freezing give it out.

## In the classroom

- **Log the temperature.** Heat water with a temperature probe in it and plot the temperature against time. The curve climbs, then flattens at the boiling point while the burner keeps going.
- **Where does the energy go?** Ask students where the burner's energy is going once the temperature stops rising. It leads naturally to latent heat.
- **Evaporative cooling.** Put a drop of water on the back of one hand and blow on both hands. The wet one feels colder: the fastest molecules leave, taking energy with them. A drop of hand sanitiser, which evaporates faster, feels colder still. (Keep sanitiser well away from any flame or hotplate.)
- **Language.** "The burner heats the water; boiling carries energy away."
- **Where it fits.** Changes of state in Years 7 to 10; latent heat in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers meet it with intermolecular forces.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Cooking pasta.** Once the water is at a rolling boil, turning the hob up further doesn't cook the pasta any faster. The water is no hotter; it just boils away faster.
- **Cooking at altitude.** Many food packets give separate instructions for high altitude. Water boils at a lower temperature there, so food takes longer to cook. A pressure cooker does the opposite, raising the boiling point to around 120 °C.
- **Steam burns.** Gram for gram, steam at 100 °C scalds worse than water at 100 °C: as each gram condenses on the skin, it releases about 2,260 joules, before it even starts cooling.

## Going deeper

**Superheating.** Water can be heated above its boiling point without boiling if there is nowhere for bubbles to start, such as in a very clean, smooth cup in a microwave oven. Disturb it (by adding a spoon of coffee, for example) and it can erupt violently. Health authorities warn about this when heating water in a microwave.

**On top of Everest.** At the summit of Mount Everest, where the air pressure is about a third of that at sea level, water boils at around 70 °C. Tea brewed there is noticeably weaker, and rice or pasta cooks very slowly, which is why climbers use pressure cookers.

**Frost protection.** Growers sometimes spray fruit trees and vines with water on frosty nights. As the water freezes on the buds, it releases energy, holding the buds at about 0 °C, warmer than the freezing air around them, so long as the spraying continues.
