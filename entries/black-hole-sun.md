## Question

Imagine the Sun were suddenly replaced by a black hole with exactly the same mass as the Sun.

Ignoring the loss of sunlight, what would happen to the Earth's orbit?

## Option

The Earth would spiral inwards and be swallowed by the black hole.

> This is the image from films: a black hole as a cosmic drain. But at the Earth's distance, the black hole's gravity would be exactly the same as the Sun's, because outside a round body, gravity depends only on its mass and the distance from its centre. The Earth would keep orbiting as it does now.

## Option (correct)

The Earth would keep orbiting just as it does now.

> Right. Outside a round body, gravity depends only on its mass and the distance from its centre, not on its size. A black hole with the Sun's mass would pull on the Earth exactly as the Sun does. (We would, of course, freeze in the dark.)

## Option

The Earth would keep orbiting, but faster, because a black hole pulls harder.

> A black hole's gravity is no stronger than that of a star of the same mass at the same distance. The pull on the Earth depends on the black hole's mass and the distance to it, and neither has changed, so the Earth would go round at the same speed as before, taking a year for each orbit.

## Option

The Earth would be torn apart by the black hole's gravity.

> Black holes do tear things apart, but only things that come very close. At the Earth's distance, 150 million km, the black hole's gravity, and the stretching it causes, would be just what the Sun's is now.

## Why it's tempting

The word "hole" suggests a drain, something that draws things in. Films and documentaries show black holes swallowing spaceships, planets and stars, and news stories describe them "devouring" their surroundings.

The one thing everybody knows about black holes, that not even light can escape, sounds like a statement about unlimited pulling power, reaching out across space.

And the most dramatic thing about them, their extreme gravity close in, is easily imagined as extending everywhere.

## A better picture

**Outside, only mass and distance matter.** Newton's law of gravitation, $F = GMm/r^2$, depends on the mass of the attracting body and the distance from its centre. For a round body, its size doesn't appear at all. Squeeze the Sun down to a ball 6 km across and the Earth, 150 million km away, would feel exactly the same pull.

**What's different is how close you can get.** With an ordinary star, you reach its surface long before gravity becomes extreme. At the Sun's surface, gravity is about 28 times as strong as at the Earth's surface. A black hole packs the same mass into a tiny space, so you can get much closer to its centre, where the pull is enormous. Only close in does a black hole behave differently from a star.

**Orbits are normal.** Stars orbit the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, which has about four million times the Sun's mass, on ordinary elliptical orbits. One of them, a star called S2, goes round in about 16 years. Tracking those orbits is how the black hole's mass was measured, work recognised by the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

**"Nothing escapes" applies only inside.** The event horizon is the boundary from inside which nothing, not even light, can ever get out. (A simple Newtonian calculation, setting the escape speed equal to the speed of light, happens to give the right radius.) For a black hole with the Sun's mass, it has a radius of only about 3 km. Outside it, escape is possible, though close to the horizon it becomes very hard.

## In the classroom

- **The replacement question.** Ask students to predict what would happen to the Earth, then look at $F = GMm/r^2$ together and ask which quantity in it has changed.
- **Calculate a Schwarzschild radius.** The radius of the event horizon is $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. Students can work it out for the Sun (about 3 km) and for the Earth (about 9 mm), and see how small a black hole is compared with the object it replaces.
- **Compare the pulls.** Work out the strength of gravity at the Sun's surface, and at 30 km from a black hole of the same mass (Newton's law is only a rough guide this close). The difference comes entirely from the distance.
- **Where it fits.** Gravity in Years 9 and 10; gravitational fields and astrophysics in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Headlines.** News stories often describe black holes "devouring" or "swallowing" nearby stars. Some do, but only stars that stray very close, or gas that gradually loses its orbital motion, through friction in a disc, and spirals in.
- **The first images.** In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole's surroundings, at the centre of the galaxy M87, and in 2022 an image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy.
- **Everyday speech.** "A black hole" is a common phrase for anything that endlessly absorbs money or time, a small daily reinforcement of the idea.

## Going deeper

**Stretched or not.** Close to a small black hole, gravity changes so quickly with distance that an object falling in would be stretched lengthways and squeezed sideways, sometimes called spaghettification. For a black hole with a few times the Sun's mass, this would tear a person apart well before they reached the event horizon. For a supermassive black hole, the stretching at the horizon is gentle, and you could cross it without noticing anything at first.

**No stable orbits too close.** Around a non-spinning black hole, there are no stable circular orbits closer than three times the radius of the event horizon. Inside that, a circular orbit is unstable: the slightest nudge sends the orbiting object plunging in. This is why the disc of gas around a black hole has an inner edge. This is one of the ways a black hole's gravity departs from Newton's law, close in.

**Black holes should evaporate, very slowly.** In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that black holes should emit a faint radiation and slowly lose mass. For a black hole with the Sun's mass, the radiation is so feeble that evaporating completely would take around $10^{67}$ years, and it can't even start yet: such a black hole is colder than the faint microwave glow left over from the Big Bang, so for now it absorbs more than it emits. Hawking radiation has not yet been detected.
