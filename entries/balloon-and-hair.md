## Question

You rub a balloon on your hair. Immediately afterwards, the balloon has a negative electric charge.

What can you say about your hair?

## Option (correct)

It has gained a positive charge, equal in size to the balloon's.

> Right. Rubbing transferred negative charge from your hair to the balloon, leaving your hair with an equal amount of positive charge. Charge can't be created or destroyed, only moved, so the two end up equal and opposite. Opposite charges attract, which is why your hair is drawn towards the balloon.

## Option

It is uncharged: the rubbing made new charge on the balloon.

> Rubbing feels like making something, the way it makes your hands warm. But charge can't be created. The balloon's negative charge came from your hair, which is left with an equal positive charge: that is why your hair stands up and reaches towards the balloon.

## Option

It has a negative charge too, like the balloon.

> If your hair had the same kind of charge as the balloon, the two would repel. But the balloon's negative charge came from your hair, so your hair is left positive, and opposite charges attract.

## Option

It has gained a positive charge, but smaller than the balloon's.

> The hair is positive, but the amounts are equal. Charge is conserved: the charge your hair lost is equal to the charge the balloon gained. (Over time, some charge may leak away to the air or through your body, but straight after rubbing, the two match.)

## Why it's tempting

Rubbing really does produce an effect you can feel: warmth, as the work you do warms the surfaces. Rub your hands together and they get hot. It is natural to think rubbing produces charge in the same way.

The school phrase "charging by friction" encourages the idea that friction makes the charge.

And we usually pay attention only to the object we were interested in, the balloon. The cloth or the hair it was rubbed on is quietly put down, and nobody checks whether it has become charged too.

## A better picture

**Everything is full of charge.** All materials contain enormous amounts of positive and negative charge, normally in exact balance, so the material as a whole is neutral.

**Contact moves charge.** When two different materials touch and are pulled apart, some charge moves from one to the other: often electrons, though with insulators such as rubber and hair it may also be charged atoms or tiny fragments of material, and researchers still argue about which. One material ends up with a surplus of negative charge, and the other with an equal deficit, which makes it positive.

**Charge is conserved.** The total amount of electric charge never changes; it can only be moved around. So the negative charge the balloon gains is exactly equal in size to the positive charge left on the hair.

**Rubbing helps, but isn't essential.** Rubbing brings many more points of the two surfaces into contact and separates them again, so more charge moves. But simply touching two materials together and pulling them apart is enough to transfer some.

**Which way?** Which material ends up positive depends on the pair. Materials can be listed in a *triboelectric series*: hair, glass, nylon and wool tend to become positive, while rubber, balloons and most plastics tend to become negative.

## In the classroom

- **Test both objects.** Charge an electroscope with a rod of known sign. Bring the balloon near: the leaves open further. Bring the cloth it was rubbed on near: the leaves fall, showing the opposite sign. (Attraction alone proves nothing, since a charged object also attracts neutral ones; only repulsion shows like charge.)
- **Sticky tape.** Stick one strip of tape on the bench and a second strip on top of it. Run a finger along them to remove any charge, peel the pair off together, then pull them apart quickly. The strips attract each other: they now carry opposite charges, without any rubbing.
- **Language.** Say "the balloon gained negative charge from your hair" rather than "the balloon was charged by rubbing". It keeps the hair in the story.
- **Where it fits.** Static electricity in Years 7 to 10; electric charge and fields in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers meet charge transfer with ions.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Door handle shocks.** Walk across a carpet in rubber-soled shoes, and charge is transferred between your shoes and the carpet with every step. Touch a metal door handle, and the charge you have built up jumps across as a small spark.
- **Clingy laundry.** Clothes tumbling against each other in a dryer transfer charge between different fabrics, which is why they cling together afterwards.
- **Thunderclouds.** The leading explanation for thunderstorm charging is that collisions between graupel (soft hail pellets) and small ice crystals, in the presence of supercooled water droplets, transfer charge. Updrafts then separate the charge on an enormous scale. The result is lightning.

## Going deeper

**Still a research problem.** That charge moves when materials touch is not in doubt. Exactly what moves, electrons, ions or tiny fragments of material, and why, is still an active research question for many pairs of materials. Even two pieces of the same material can charge each other when rubbed.

**How well is charge conserved?** Conservation of charge is one of the most precisely tested laws in physics. One test looks for the electron decaying into lighter particles, which would destroy its charge: none has ever been seen. The Borexino experiment in Italy showed that if the electron decays into a neutrino and a photon, its average lifetime must be at least about $10^{28}$ years, over a billion billion times the age of the universe.

**Harvesting static.** Devices called triboelectric nanogenerators turn the charge separated by touching and pulling apart into small electrical currents, and are being developed to power sensors from footsteps, vibrations and the movement of clothing.
