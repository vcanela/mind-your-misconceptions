## Question

Could the reactor of an ordinary nuclear power station blow up like a nuclear bomb, if things went badly wrong?

## Option

Yes: a reactor is a bomb kept under control, so losing control sets it off.

> Both do use nuclear fission, so the idea is natural. But a bomb needs highly enriched material, assembled in a tiny fraction of a second, so that the chain reaction multiplies explosively before the material blows itself apart. Reactor fuel is only a few percent uranium-235, and its chain reaction grows far too slowly: if it runs away, the heat bursts the core apart long before anything like a nuclear explosion can build up.

## Option

Yes, if all of its control rods were pulled out at once.

> Withdrawing control rods can make a reactor's chain reaction run away, and Chernobyl's reactor ran away in 1986 after most of its control rods had been withdrawn, in a reactor with serious design flaws. But the result was a huge surge of heat that flashed the cooling water to steam and blew the reactor apart: a steam explosion, not a nuclear one.

## Option

No: the reactor's thick steel and concrete would contain the blast.

> A reactor's thick walls help contain radioactive material in an accident, but they couldn't hold back a nuclear explosion, and they aren't what prevents one. What prevents it is the physics of the chain reaction: in reactor fuel, it can't grow fast enough to become a nuclear explosion.

## Option (correct)

No: its chain reaction can't grow fast enough to explode like that.

> Right. A bomb needs uranium enriched to about 90% uranium-235, or plutonium, assembled in a tiny fraction of a second. With no moderator, each generation of the chain reaction takes about a hundred-millionth of a second, and the whole reaction is over in about a millionth. Reactor fuel is only a few percent uranium-235, and its chain reaction is far slower, held in check partly by neutrons released seconds after each fission. If it runs away, the core heats up and bursts apart long before a nuclear explosion could build up.

## Why it's tempting

Bombs and reactors really do share a process: both release energy by splitting heavy nuclei in a chain reaction. It seems to follow that a reactor is simply a bomb held in check.

Nuclear accidents are frightening, and they do involve real explosions. Pictures of the shattered reactor building at Chernobyl, or the exploding buildings at Fukushima, look like confirmation.

And the single word "nuclear" ties together weapons, power stations, waste and fallout in public discussion, so that a danger of one is easily assumed to be a danger of all.

## A better picture

**A bomb is a race.** In a nuclear weapon, a mass of highly enriched fissile material, typically about 90% uranium-235, or plutonium-239, is assembled in a tiny fraction of a second. Each fission releases neutrons that cause further fissions almost at once, and the chain reaction multiplies through generations lasting about a hundred-millionth of a second each. It has to release its energy before the heat blows the material apart and stops the reaction.

**A reactor is too slow to be a bomb.** Most power reactor fuel is only about 3 to 5% uranium-235; the rest is uranium-238, which can't sustain a chain reaction by itself. Most reactors slow their neutrons down with a moderator, and every power reactor is run so that it relies on a small fraction of neutrons that are released seconds after each fission, which keeps the chain reaction slow enough to control. Prompt neutron generations in a water-cooled reactor last around a ten-thousandth of a second, and in graphite or heavy-water reactors about a thousandth; with the delayed neutrons included, the chain reaction responds over seconds.

**What goes wrong instead.** Reactor accidents are real, and can be devastating, but their explosions are of other kinds:

- **Chernobyl (1986).** The chain reaction ran away, and the power surged to many times the reactor's normal output in seconds, flashing the cooling water to steam. A steam explosion blew the reactor open, and the explosions and the fires that followed released radioactive material for about ten days, spreading it across much of Europe.
- **Fukushima (2011).** After the tsunami knocked out the cooling, the overheating fuel cladding reacted with steam to produce hydrogen, which exploded in the reactor buildings.

The lasting harm comes from radioactive material released into the environment, not from a nuclear blast.

## In the classroom

- **Compare the time scales.** The decisive difference is how fast the neutrons multiply: each generation takes about a hundred-millionth of a second in a bomb, against about a ten-thousandth of a second in a water-cooled reactor. And in a bomb each generation roughly doubles the neutrons, while in a reactor, even in an accident, each generation adds only a fraction of a percent.
- **Ask what exploded.** Take Chernobyl and Fukushima and identify the explosions: steam and hydrogen. Both serious; neither nuclear.
- **Keep the real risks visible.** Correcting the misconception is not a claim that reactors are safe in every respect. It puts the risk in the right place: loss of cooling, fires, and the release of radioactive material.
- **Where it fits.** Nuclear energy in Years 9 and 10; fission and chain reactions in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Cooling towers.** The white plumes from the huge cooling towers of some power stations, nuclear or not, are water vapour condensing into droplets, like a cloud. They aren't smoke, and they aren't radioactive.
- **Films and games.** Stories often feature reactors that can "go critical" and explode like bombs. In fact a reactor is "critical" all the time it is running steadily: the word simply means its chain reaction is self-sustaining.
- **News coverage.** Reports often say a reactor "exploded". At Chernobyl the reactor itself was blown apart; at Fukushima it was the buildings around the reactors. In neither case was the explosion nuclear.

## Going deeper

**Delayed neutrons.** Most neutrons from a fission are released almost instantly, but a small fraction, under 1% for uranium-235, come out seconds later, from the decay of fission fragments. A power reactor is run so that its chain reaction can only grow with the help of those delayed neutrons, released seconds to about a minute after each fission, which slows any change in power to a pace that control systems can follow. Chernobyl is what happens when that margin is lost: the reactor went "prompt critical" and its power rose about a hundredfold in seconds. Even then, the core blew itself apart by steam pressure long before anything like a nuclear explosion could build up.

**After Fukushima.** In its assessments of the Fukushima accident, the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation concluded that no adverse health effects among the public are expected to be discernible as a result of the radiation. Much of the harm to health came instead from the disruption and stress of the evacuation.

**A natural reactor.** About two billion years ago, at Oklo in what is now Gabon, natural deposits of uranium, richer in uranium-235 than today's and soaked in groundwater, sustained fission chain reactions on and off for hundreds of thousands of years. They never exploded: when the water boiled away, the reactions slowed down by themselves.
