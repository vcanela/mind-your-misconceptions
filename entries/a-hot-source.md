## Question

A sealed sample of radioactive cobalt-60 is heated in a furnace, from room temperature to 1000 °C.

What happens to its half-life?

## Option

It gets shorter, because heating speeds up reactions.

> This is a reasonable guess from chemistry, where heating speeds reactions up enormously. But radioactive decay happens inside the nucleus, governed by energies millions of times larger than anything heating can supply. At 1000 °C, the half-life is unchanged.

## Option

It no longer applies: heating destroys the radioactivity.

> Heating can melt, burn or vaporise a radioactive material, changing its chemical form, but the unstable nuclei are untouched, and they keep decaying at the same rate. Burning radioactive material can even make things worse, by spreading it in smoke.

## Option (correct)

It stays the same: heat is far too weak to affect the nucleus.

> Right. Heat, pressure and chemistry act on the electrons outside the nucleus, with energies of a fraction of an electronvolt. Radioactive decay is governed by forces inside the nucleus, with energies of thousands to millions of electronvolts. Heating can't reach in far enough, or push hard enough, to change the half-life.

## Option

It stays the same, because the sample is sealed and nothing can escape.

> The right result, for a reason that doesn't hold. Sealing keeps radioactive material from escaping, but it has nothing to do with the half-life: an unsealed sample heated to 1000 °C would decay at exactly the same rate. The half-life stays the same because heat is far too weak to affect the nucleus.

## Why it's tempting

In chemistry, temperature matters enormously: heating a reaction usually speeds it up, often dramatically. It is natural to assume radioactive decay, often described as a nuclear "reaction", behaves the same way.

We also think of heat as a way of destroying things: fire burns, and cooking kills germs. It seems reasonable that heat might destroy radioactivity too.

And it seems unlikely that anything could be completely unaffected by the conditions around it.

## A better picture

**The nucleus is shielded by scale.** Radioactive decay is governed by the forces inside the nucleus, with energies of thousands to millions of electronvolts. Temperature, pressure and chemistry act on the electrons outside, with energies of electronvolts or less. At room temperature, the typical energy of thermal motion is about 0.025 eV; even at 1000 °C it is only about 0.1 eV, tens of thousands to millions of times too small to make any difference.

**So half-lives are constant.** Experiments have heated, cooled, compressed and chemically combined radioactive materials, and found no significant change in most decay rates. That is why each isotope has a fixed half-life, and why radioactive dating works.

**The honest exceptions.** A few kinds of decay involve the atom's own electrons, and these can be nudged slightly:

- **Electron capture.** Some nuclei decay by capturing one of the atom's inner electrons, so the chemical surroundings can change the rate slightly: for beryllium-7, by about 1% (up to 1.5% in the most extreme case measured).
- **Stripped atoms.** Strip a heavy atom of all its electrons, which happens naturally only deep inside stars, and on Earth only in particle accelerators, and some decays change dramatically. Fully stripped rhenium-187, whose ordinary half-life is about 42 billion years, decays in about 33 years.

Neither makes any practical difference to radioactive waste or to radioactive dating on Earth: the electron-capture effects are tiny, and fully stripped heavy atoms don't occur here naturally.

## In the classroom

- **Compare the energies.** A fraction of an electronvolt for heat, a few electronvolts for chemistry, millions for the nucleus: the gap shows why the two worlds barely touch.
- **Ask why dating works.** If decay rates changed with temperature, rocks from hot and cold places would give inconsistent ages. Different radioactive clocks, in different rocks, agree.
- **Mention the exceptions.** Telling students there are tiny exceptions, and why, is more convincing than an absolute claim, and it shows how physics handles its own limits.
- **Where it fits.** Radioactivity in Years 9 and 10; nuclear physics in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers can use it to contrast nuclear and chemical change.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"Burning" nuclear waste.** Proposals to "burn" long-lived radioactive waste don't mean setting fire to it. They mean bombarding it with neutrons in a reactor, changing the nuclei themselves into shorter-lived ones.
- **Fires and radioactivity.** Wildfires in contaminated areas, such as the forests around Chernobyl, don't destroy the radioactive material; they can lift it into the air and spread it.
- **Radioactive dating.** Ages from radiocarbon and other radioactive clocks rely on half-lives being unaffected by the conditions the samples have been through.

## Going deeper

**Early tests.** In the decades after radioactivity was discovered, physicists tried hard to change decay rates. Rutherford heated radon to about 2500 °C in an explosion, and in 1913 Marie Curie and Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled radium to the temperature of liquid hydrogen, about 20 K. Nothing they tried made any measurable difference.

**A nuclear clock.** Thorium-229 has an unusual excited nuclear state, only about 8 electronvolts above its ground state: low enough to be reached with vacuum-ultraviolet laser light. Its lifetime depends enormously on its electronic surroundings, from microseconds in a neutral atom to about half an hour in a crystal, making it the most dramatic exception of all. In 2024 physicists excited it with lasers for the first time, a step towards nuclear clocks that could be even more precise than today's atomic clocks.

**Claims of seasonal variation.** Some studies have reported tiny seasonal variations in decay rates, suggesting a link with the Earth's distance from the Sun. Careful experiments designed to test the claim have not confirmed it; the variations are generally attributed to effects on the detectors.
