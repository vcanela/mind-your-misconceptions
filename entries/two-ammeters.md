## Question

A cell is connected to a single bulb, which lights up. There are two ammeters in the circuit, one on each side of the bulb.

![A cell and a bulb in a single loop. Ammeter 1 is in the wire from the cell's positive terminal to the bulb; ammeter 2 is in the wire from the bulb back to the cell.](figures/two-ammeters.svg)

Ammeter 1, in the wire from the cell's positive terminal to the bulb, reads 0.20 A. What does ammeter 2, in the wire from the bulb back to the cell, read?

## Option

More than 0.20 A

> Perhaps you were thinking of electrons, which flow out of the negative terminal. Then ammeter 2 is the one they reach first, before the bulb, so it seems it should read more. That is the same idea as c, seen from the other direction. In fact the direction makes no difference: both ammeters read the same.

## Option (correct)

Exactly 0.20 A

> Every bit of charge that flows into the bulb flows out of it again: there is nowhere else for it to go. Current is the rate at which charge passes a point, and since charge isn't piling up anywhere, it is the same everywhere in a single loop. What the bulb takes is energy.

## Option

Less than 0.20 A, but more than zero

> This is one of the most common wrong answers, and it turns up at every age, from primary school to university. It pictures current as a kind of fuel: the bulb burns some of it, and less comes out the other side. But current is moving charge, and charge isn't used up. If less came out of the bulb than went in, charge would be piling up inside it, and it isn't. Something is used up in the bulb, though: energy.

## Option

Zero: the bulb uses all of the current

> This pictures current flowing out of the cell to the bulb and being used up there, so the return wire carries nothing. If that were so, the return wire would be unnecessary. Yet disconnect it and the bulb goes out at once. The current needs a complete loop because it goes all the way round.

## Why it's tempting

Something really is used up when a bulb lights, so the instinct isn't foolish: it is aimed at the wrong quantity. We pay for electricity, we "use" it, and batteries run out. It is natural to assume that the thing flowing in the wires is the thing being consumed.

The words don't help. *Current* suggests a stream of stuff, like water, and water flowing from a tap does get used. We talk about a battery "running out of charge" and about "charging" a phone, when what runs down is stored energy.

Picturing the circuit as a one-way delivery, from cell to bulb, also makes the second wire look like a return path that carries only the leftovers.

## A better picture

**Charge goes round.** The wires and the bulb filament are full of charged particles (free electrons) before the cell is ever connected. The cell sets them all moving at once, all the way round the loop. (Inside the cell, the charge is carried by ions rather than electrons, but the current there is the same too.) None are created in the cell, and none are destroyed in the bulb.

**Current is a rate.** Current is the amount of charge passing a point each second, $I = Q/t$. In a single loop, the same amount of charge passes every point each second, so the current is the same everywhere: before the bulb, inside it, and after it.

**Energy is what gets used.** The cell gives energy to the charge passing through it, and the bulb transfers that energy to light and heating. The potential difference measures the energy per unit of charge: a 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb of charge passing through it 1.5 joules.

**A bicycle chain.** A chain carries energy from the pedals to the back wheel. Every link goes round, none are used up, and the chain moves at the same rate everywhere. What travels from pedals to wheel is energy, not chain.

## In the classroom

- **Predict, then measure.** Have students write down predictions for both ammeters before connecting anything, then measure. The equal readings are often a genuine surprise.
- **Swap the meters.** Small differences between real meters will be seized on as evidence that current is used up. Swap the two ammeters over: if the difference moves with the meter, it belongs to the meter, not to the circuit.
- **The rope loop.** Pass a loop of rope round the class, held loosely in everyone's hands. One student (the cell) pulls it round; another grips it lightly (the bulb) and feels their hands warm up. The rope is the charge: it all keeps moving, and none of it is used up. The warmth is the energy.
- **Language.** Say "the cell pushes charge round" and "the bulb transfers energy". Avoid "the bulb uses current", and where you can, "the battery has run out of charge".
- **Where it fits.** First circuits, anywhere in Years 7 to 10. It often survives into Year 11 and beyond, especially in circuits with several components.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The power bill.** What you pay for is energy, in kilowatt-hours. Not one electron is sold: in a mains supply, the same electrons in the wires just jiggle back and forth.
- **Safety switches.** A residual current device (RCD) compares the current going out along the live wire with the current coming back along the neutral. Normally they are equal, which is exactly the point of this question. If they differ, some current is taking another route, perhaps through a person, and the RCD cuts the power in a fraction of a second.
- **"Running out of charge."** A flat phone battery contains exactly as much charge as a full one, and is electrically neutral either way. What it has run out of is stored energy: the ability to push charge round a circuit. (The mAh figure on a battery measures how much charge it can push round before it goes flat, which is partly why the word "charge" sticks.)

## Going deeper

**Slow electrons, instant light.** The free electrons in the connecting wires drift along at well under a millimetre per second, yet the bulb lights the moment the switch closes. The whole loop of charge starts moving at once, and the energy reaches the bulb through the electric and magnetic fields around the wires, not by riding along with the electrons.

**Junctions.** Where a circuit branches, the current splits, but the total current into a junction always equals the total out (Kirchhoff's current law). It is the same rule, conservation of charge, applied at a point.

**Where it bends.** With a capacitor in a circuit, charge does pile up, on the plates. Even then, the current flowing onto one plate equals the current flowing off the other. And in circuits that change very quickly or are very long, such as radio antennas or long transmission lines, the current at different points along the same wire at the same instant can genuinely differ, because charge bunches up as the signal travels along.
