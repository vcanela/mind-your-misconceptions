## Question

In its lowest energy state, how is the electron in a hydrogen atom best described?

## Option

A tiny ball circling the nucleus in a fixed orbit, like a planet.

> This is the Bohr model of 1913, and the familiar atom symbol. It correctly gives hydrogen's energy levels, but its orbits aren't real. An electron in an orbit would have both a definite position and a definite momentum, which quantum mechanics rules out. And an orbiting charge should radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second.

## Option (correct)

A standing wave spread around the nucleus, with no definite path.

> Right. In quantum mechanics, the bound electron is described by a wavefunction: a three-dimensional standing wave around the nucleus. In the lowest state it is spherical. It doesn't describe a path; it tells you how likely the electron is to be found in each region, if you look.

## Option

A tiny ball moving very fast along an unknown, random path.

> A common refinement: the "electron cloud" pictured as the blur of a fast electron zipping around. But there is no path, random or otherwise, for the electron to follow. The cloud describes how likely the electron is to be found in each region if you look, not the trace of a hidden journey.

## Option

A standing wave running round a circle, a whole number of wavelengths long.

> This is de Broglie's 1924 picture, which explains Bohr's orbits, but it is still an orbit at a fixed radius. In the lowest state the electron has no orbital angular momentum at all: the wave is spherical, with no circulation, and it isn't confined to one distance.

## Why it's tempting

The picture of an atom as a tiny solar system is everywhere: in logos, on signs, in emoji and in textbooks. It is the image most people carry of what an atom looks like.

The Bohr model, with electrons in circular orbits, was a real scientific advance, and it is still taught as a stepping stone because it predicts hydrogen's energy levels correctly.

And the word "orbital", used for the states of electrons in chemistry, sounds almost exactly like "orbit".

## A better picture

**No paths.** In quantum mechanics, an electron in an atom doesn't follow a trajectory. It is described by a wavefunction, $\psi$, and $|\psi|^2$ gives the probability per unit volume of finding the electron near each point. The wavefunctions, and the probability clouds drawn from them, are called orbitals, not orbits.

**Standing waves.** A bound electron behaves like a three-dimensional standing wave, confined by the attraction of the nucleus. Just as a guitar string can only vibrate in certain patterns, only certain wave patterns fit around a nucleus. Each pattern has a definite energy, and only certain energies are possible, which is why atoms have discrete energy levels and give out light only at particular wavelengths.

**Why orbits fail.** An orbit would need the electron to have a definite position and a definite momentum at every moment, which the uncertainty principle rules out. And an orbiting charge is accelerating, so classical physics says it should radiate light, lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second. A standing wave's charge distribution doesn't change with time, so it has no classical reason to radiate, and the lowest state has no lower state to drop to. That is why atoms are stable.

**The Bohr model's place.** Bohr's formula for hydrogen's energy levels, $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$, is correct, and discrete energy levels are real. The orbits are what has been discarded.

## In the classroom

- **Standing waves first.** Show standing waves on a string, or the patterns on a vibrating plate, before introducing orbitals. Only certain patterns fit, which is the heart of quantisation. (If you use a vibrating ring, say explicitly that a real atom's standing waves fill three-dimensional space and are not waves running round a circle.)
- **Orbital pictures.** Show images of hydrogen's orbitals: the spherical s orbitals and the dumbbell-shaped p orbitals. They are the correct pictures, and they are striking.
- **Acknowledge Bohr.** Present the Bohr model as a useful historical model that gets the energies right, not as a description of what the electron is doing.
- **Language.** Say "orbital" rather than "orbit". "Shell" is fine for a group of states with the same $n$, as long as it isn't pictured as a track.
- **Where it fits.** Atomic structure from Year 10; quantum ideas in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma. Chemistry teachers meet orbitals in bonding.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The atom symbol.** The familiar emblem of electrons on elliptical orbits around a nucleus appears on logos and signs worldwide, and in the atom emoji.
- **"Orbitals."** Chemistry's word for electron states keeps the echo of the old orbit picture alive.
- **Pictures of orbitals.** In 2013 physicists used a "quantum microscope" to make the first direct images of the nodal pattern of an excited hydrogen atom's wavefunction, by magnifying the electrons it released when ionised in an electric field.

## Going deeper

**Where is the electron most likely?** In hydrogen's lowest state, the probability per unit volume, $|\psi|^2$, is actually greatest right at the nucleus. But there is very little volume close to the nucleus, so the most likely distance at which to find the electron is about 0.053 nm, the Bohr radius.

**Inside the nucleus.** Because the probability doesn't vanish at the centre, there is a tiny probability, a few parts in $10^{15}$ for hydrogen, of finding an s electron inside the nucleus itself. That is what makes electron capture possible: some nuclei decay by absorbing one of their own electrons, which is why the chemical surroundings of beryllium-7 can slightly change its half-life (see [[two-half-lives]]).
