## Question

On a warm day, in a room at 25 °C, two identical ice cubes are taken out of the freezer and put on two plates side by side. One ice cube is wrapped snugly in a woollen scarf; the other is left uncovered.

Which ice cube melts completely first?

## Option (correct)

The uncovered one

> Right. Energy flows into both ice cubes from the warmer room. The scarf traps a layer of still air round the wrapped cube, and still air conducts energy very poorly, so energy reaches that cube more slowly and it lasts longer.

## Option

The wrapped one

> You probably reasoned that the scarf is warm, so it would melt the ice faster. That comes from real experience: wrap a scarf round your neck and you feel warmer. But that warmth comes from you. A scarf has no source of energy of its own; left in a 25 °C room, it is at 25 °C, the same as the air. What it does is slow down the flow of energy. Round your neck, it slows the energy leaving your body. Round an ice cube, it slows the energy reaching the ice.

## Option

Both at the same time

> Perhaps you reasoned that a scarf isn't warm in itself, which is true. But it does make a difference. It slows the flow of energy in either direction: out of a warm body, or into a cold ice cube. Here the energy is flowing in, so the wrapped cube melts more slowly.

## Why it's tempting

The experience is vivid and immediate: put on a jumper or climb under a duvet and you feel warmer within moments. It is natural to conclude that the warmth comes from the wool.

Language backs it up. We talk about "warm clothes", "a warm jumper" and "a cool cotton shirt", labelling materials by how they feel to wear rather than by what they do.

Touch backs it up too. A woollen blanket feels warm to the touch and a metal spoon feels cold, even when both have been sitting in the same room at the same temperature. Our skin senses its own temperature, not the temperature of what it touches. Metal draws energy out of your fingers quickly, so the skin cools and the metal feels cold; wool draws very little, so the skin stays warm and the wool feels warm.

Underneath is often the idea that cold is a substance: something the scarf could keep out, or that the freezer lets out when its door is opened.

## A better picture

**There is only one thing flowing, and it is energy.** Energy flows by itself from hotter places to colder ones. There is no "cold" that moves the other way: when a freezer door is opened, energy flows from the kitchen into the freezer.

**A scarf has no energy source.** Anything left in a room for long enough ends up at the temperature of the room. A scarf in a 25 °C room is at 25 °C, as a thermometer wrapped inside it will show.

**Insulators slow the flow.** Still air is a very poor conductor of energy, but air left free to move carries energy away by convection. The fibres of wool, fleece or down hold a thick layer of air still, and that is most of what makes them good insulators.

**Direction doesn't matter.** Round a warm body, insulation slows the loss of the energy your body is constantly releasing (roughly 100 W at rest), so your skin stays warmer. Round an ice cube, it slows the energy flowing in from the room, so the ice stays frozen longer. The scarf does the same job in both cases.

## In the classroom

- **Do the experiment.** It needs only ice cubes, a scarf or woollen sock, and patience. Ask students to predict and give reasons first; the predictions are revealing. Adding a third cube wrapped in aluminium foil makes a good discussion.
- **Thermometer in a scarf.** Wrap a thermometer in a scarf for ten minutes. It reads room temperature, which surprises many students.
- **Metal and wood.** Have students touch a metal block and a wooden block that have been on the same bench, then measure both with a contact thermometer probe (an infrared thermometer can misread shiny metal unless it is covered with matt tape). Same temperature, very different feel.
- **Language.** Say "the scarf keeps energy in" or "keeps energy out", rather than "keeps you warm" or "keeps the cold out".
- **Where it fits.** Heat transfer in Years 7 to 10. Chemistry teachers meet the same idea with endothermic reactions, and biology teachers with body temperature.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The chilly bin.** The same insulated box keeps drinks cold at the beach and keeps takeaways hot on the way home. It isn't cold or hot in itself: it slows energy flow in both directions.
- **The ice trade.** Before refrigeration, ice was cut from frozen lakes in winter and stored for summer in ice houses, packed in sawdust or straw. In the 1800s, Frederic Tudor of Boston shipped ice packed in sawdust as far as India.
- **Cold floors.** In the same room, a tiled floor feels much colder underfoot than a carpet, although both are at the same temperature.

## Going deeper

**Numbers for still air.** The thermal conductivity of still air is about 0.026 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, while that of water is about 0.6 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, more than twenty times higher, and that of copper about 400 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. That is why wet clothing loses much of its insulating power, and why a diver's wetsuit uses a foam full of trapped gas bubbles.

**Reflecting cold?** In 1790 the Genevan physicist Marc-Auguste Pictet set up two large concave mirrors facing each other. A thermometer at the focus of one mirror got hotter when a hot object was placed at the focus of the other. Then he tried a flask of snow, and the thermometer got colder: cold, it seemed, could be reflected. The general explanation came from his fellow Genevan Pierre Prévost in 1791, in his theory of exchanges. Every object radiates energy all the time, and the thermometer was radiating more energy towards the snow than it received back from it. No cold travelled anywhere; the thermometer simply had a net loss.

**The end of caloric.** In the 18th century heat was widely thought to be a weightless fluid called caloric, flowing from hot bodies to cold ones. In 1798, Count Rumford reported that boring cannon barrels produced heat apparently without limit, as long as the boring continued, which a conserved fluid could not do. In the 1840s James Joule measured how much mechanical work produces a given amount of heating, and heat came to be understood as a transfer of energy.
