## Question

A horse is pulling a cart along a level road, and the two are speeding up. Through the harness, the horse pulls forwards on the cart, and the cart pulls backwards on the horse. (The harness is light compared with the horse and the cart.)

While they are speeding up, how does the cart's backward pull on the horse compare with the horse's forward pull on the cart?

## Option

The horse's pull is bigger; otherwise they couldn't speed up.

> This is the natural way to explain the speeding up, and most students reason this way before being taught otherwise. A subtler version says the pulls are equal at a steady speed but not while speeding up. But the two pulls are equal at every instant, whatever the horse and cart are doing: they are the two halves of a single interaction, passed along by the light harness. The speeding up is explained by other forces. The cart speeds up because the horse's pull on it is bigger than the friction on the cart, and the horse speeds up because the ground's forward push on its hooves is bigger than the cart's backward pull.

## Option (correct)

The two pulls are equal, even while they are speeding up.

> Right. Through the light harness, the horse and cart pull on each other with equal and opposite forces at every instant: in effect, a third-law pair. They don't cancel, because they act on different objects, one on the cart and one on the horse. Whether each object speeds up depends only on the forces acting on it.

## Option

It depends on whether the horse or the cart is heavier.

> This is the idea that the bigger or heavier object exerts the bigger force: the same instinct that says a truck hits a small car harder than the car hits the truck. But the two pulls are a single interaction, so whatever the masses, each pulls on the other equally. Mass decides how much each one speeds up in response, not how hard they pull on each other.

## Why it's tempting

"Equal and opposite" sounds exactly like the recipe for forces that cancel. We learn that two equal and opposite forces on an object add up to zero, and it seems natural to apply that here too.

We also look for a winner. Something is speeding up, so surely the stronger party is pushing harder: the horse must out-pull the cart, as one team out-pulls the other in a tug of war. The same instinct says a truck hits a small car harder than the car hits the truck.

And the old wording, "action and reaction", suggests a sequence: first the action, then the reaction, as though one came after and in answer to the other. In fact the two forces arise together and last exactly as long as each other.

## A better picture

**One interaction, two forces.** When two objects interact, each pushes or pulls on the other. The two forces are always equal in size, opposite in direction, of the same kind, and they act on different objects. That is Newton's third law.

**Ask: forces on what?** To decide whether something speeds up, add up only the forces acting on that object, and ignore any forces it exerts on other things.

![Forces on the cart and forces on the horse, drawn separately.](figures/horse-and-cart.svg "Horizontal forces only. The two pulls, P, are equal: a third-law pair. Each appears on a different diagram, so they never cancel.")

- **The cart** feels the horse's forward pull and a backward friction force on its wheels. It speeds up because the pull is bigger than the friction.
- **The horse** feels the cart's backward pull, and a forward push from the ground on its hooves (the horse pushes back on the ground; the ground pushes forward on the horse). It speeds up because the ground's push is bigger than the cart's pull.

**The whole team.** Treat horse and cart together as one object, and the pulls between them are internal: they do cancel, because now both act within the same system. What's left are the outside forces: the ground's push on the hooves forwards, and the friction on the wheels backwards. The team speeds up because the first is bigger. Everything comes down to how hard the horse can push against the road.

## In the classroom

- **Two spring balances.** Hook two spring balances (or force sensors) together and have two students pull. However they pull, and whoever pulls harder, the two readings match.
- **Chairs and a rope.** Two students sit on wheeled office chairs, with their feet off the floor, and hold a rope between them. Whichever one pulls, both chairs move towards each other.
- **Always draw separate diagrams.** One free-body diagram per object, and only forces acting on that object. The two forces of a third-law pair always end up on different diagrams.
- **Language.** Prefer "the force of the horse on the cart" and "the force of the cart on the horse", which name both objects every time. Avoid "action and reaction".
- **Where it fits.** Forces in Years 9 and 10; momentum and collisions in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Tug of war.** The winning team doesn't pull harder on the rope than the rope pulls on them. With a light rope, both teams pull on it with almost the same force. The winners are the team that pushes harder against the ground, which is why grip and leaning back matter so much.
- **Collisions.** When a truck and a small car collide, each exerts the same force on the other. The car's occupants come off worse because the car, having much less mass, undergoes a much larger change in velocity.
- **Walking.** To walk forwards, you push backwards on the ground, and the ground pushes you forwards. On ice, where the ground can push you forwards only weakly, you have to take short, careful steps; on perfectly frictionless ice you couldn't get going at all.

## Going deeper

**The third law and momentum.** Two equal and opposite forces, acting for exactly the same time, give the two objects equal and opposite changes in momentum. So forces between two objects can never change their total momentum; only outside forces can. In ordinary mechanics, Newton's third law is what guarantees the conservation of momentum.

**A heavy rope.** In a tug of war with a heavy rope that is speeding up, the pulls at the two ends of the rope are not equal: the difference is what accelerates the rope's own mass. That doesn't break the third law. The pulls at the two ends act on the same object, the rope, and are not a third-law pair. Each end's pull is paired with the rope's equal and opposite pull on that team. The same goes for a heavy harness, which is why the question specifies a light one.

**Where the simple form fails.** For two charged particles moving past each other, the magnetic forces they exert on each other are not always equal and opposite. Momentum is still conserved, because the electromagnetic field itself carries momentum, and the field's share balances the books. So conservation of momentum is the deeper law: Newton's third law, in its simple form, is how it shows up when forces act directly between objects.
