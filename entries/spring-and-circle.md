## Question

A mass bouncing up and down on a spring moves with simple harmonic motion (SHM). SHM is often explained using a point moving steadily round a circle.

What makes the mass move with simple harmonic motion?

## Option (correct)

A restoring force proportional to its distance from the middle.

> Right. Whenever the force pulling an object back towards its middle position is proportional to how far it has been displaced, $F = -kx$, the motion is simple harmonic. Newton's second law then gives $a = -\omega^2 x$, whose solutions are sine and cosine functions of time.

## Option

Circular motion, seen side-on, as in the reference circle.

> The reference circle is a useful drawing: the shadow of a point moving steadily round a circle moves in exactly the same way as the mass. But nothing in a bouncing spring goes round a circle. The circle is a way of picturing the motion, not its cause.

## Option

Gravity, which pulls it back down after each bounce.

> For a mass hanging on a spring, gravity simply shifts the middle point of the bounce downwards. It is the spring's force, changing in proportion to the displacement from that middle point, that makes the motion simple harmonic. The same mass and spring bouncing horizontally on a smooth table, with gravity playing no part, has exactly the same period.

## Option

The spring's force, which stays the same size throughout the bounce.

> A spring's force isn't constant: the further it is stretched or squashed, the harder it pulls or pushes. That changing force, proportional to the displacement from the middle, is exactly what makes the motion simple harmonic. A constant force would just accelerate the mass steadily in one direction.

## Why it's tempting

The reference circle is often the very first way SHM is presented: a point going steadily round a circle, with its shadow sliding back and forth. It is memorable, and if it comes before the force, the circle can seem like the foundation.

Sines and cosines appear in both topics, and so do angular frequency $\omega$ and phase angles, which makes the two look like the same phenomenon.

And phasor diagrams for waves and alternating current use the same rotating-arrow picture, reinforcing the idea that something is going round.

## A better picture

**SHM has a simple cause.** Simple harmonic motion happens whenever the force pulling an object back towards its equilibrium position is proportional to its displacement: $F = -kx$. Put that into Newton's second law and you get $a = -\omega^2 x$, with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, whose solutions are sine and cosine functions.

**No circle in sight.** A mass on a spring moves in a straight line. A pendulum swings back and forth along a short arc; nothing goes all the way round. What they share is the restoring force.

**The circle is a drawing aid.** Take a point moving steadily round a circle, and look at its shadow on a line. The shadow obeys exactly the same equation as SHM. That is a beautiful and useful correspondence, because it makes phase easy to see, but it is a way of picturing the motion, not a mechanism.

**The period.** For a mass on a spring, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. It doesn't depend on how far the mass bounces: a big bounce and a small one take the same time.

## In the classroom

- **Lead with the force.** Derive SHM from $F = -kx$ and Newton's second law before mentioning a circle. Then the circle is clearly an extra, not the foundation.
- **Find the circle.** Hang a mass on a spring and ask students to point to the circle. There isn't one.
- **Shadow and pendulum.** Shine a light so that the shadow of a peg on a rotating turntable falls next to the shadow of a swinging pendulum with the same period, and a swing as wide as the peg's circle. Started in step, the two shadows move together: the same motion, from two quite different causes. (At 45 rpm the period is 1.33 s, which needs a pendulum about 0.44 m long.)
- **Where it fits.** Simple harmonic motion in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Quartz watches.** A quartz watch keeps time with a tiny quartz tuning fork vibrating 32,768 times a second, a high-speed simple harmonic oscillator. No circle involved.
- **Pendulum clocks.** Christiaan Huygens invented the pendulum clock in 1656, using the fact that a pendulum's period hardly depends on how far it swings.
- **Car suspension.** A car's springs make it bounce up and down after a bump; the shock absorbers are there to damp the oscillation out quickly.

## Going deeper

**Only approximately.** A pendulum is only approximately simple harmonic: its restoring force is proportional to the sine of the angle, not the angle itself. For small swings the difference is tiny, but a pendulum swinging out to 90° takes about 18% longer per swing than a small-amplitude one.

**Why SHM is everywhere.** Near the bottom of almost any smooth energy valley, the shape is close to a parabola, and a parabolic valley gives a restoring force proportional to displacement. That is why small oscillations of almost anything, from atoms in a crystal to a footbridge swaying under walkers, are approximately simple harmonic.
