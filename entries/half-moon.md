## Question

One evening you see a half Moon (a first quarter Moon): exactly half of its disc is bright, and the other half is dark.

Why is the other half dark?

## Option

The Earth's shadow is falling on that half of the Moon.

> Shadows are the everyday way for part of something to go dark, so this feels natural. But at first quarter the Moon is off to one side of the Earth, a quarter of the way round its orbit, nowhere near the line from the Sun through the Earth, which is where the Earth's shadow falls. The shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which can happen only at full Moon.

## Option

Clouds, or the Earth's atmosphere, are blocking our view of that half.

> Clouds come and go, but at any given moment everyone on Earth who can see the Moon sees the same phase, whatever their weather (though turned at different angles). And when the Moon is a crescent you can often see the dark part faintly, lit by sunlight reflected from the Earth, which shows that nothing is blocking the view.

## Option (correct)

That half is facing away from the Sun, so it is in night-time.

> Right. The Sun always lights exactly half of the Moon, the half facing it. At first quarter the Moon is at right angles to the Sun as seen from the Earth, so we see the boundary between its day side and its night side edge-on: half of the disc we see is in daylight, and half in darkness.

## Option

That half of the Moon is covered in darker rock.

> The Moon does have darker regions: the maria, or "seas", plains of old lava that make the face of the Man in the Moon. But they are always in the same place on the disc, while the dark part of the Moon changes from night to night. The phases are about lighting, not rock.

## Why it's tempting

Shadows are the familiar way for part of an object to go dark. The dark part of the Moon really is unlit, so "something is blocking the light" seems a natural explanation, and the Earth is the obvious candidate.

Lunar eclipses, when the Earth's shadow really does darken the Moon, are memorable events, and they seem to confirm it.

Diagrams don't always help. Drawn small on a page, with the Sun, Earth and Moon all close together, it is easy to imagine the Earth getting in the way.

## A better picture

**The Moon makes no light of its own.** We see it by reflected sunlight, and at every moment the Sun lights exactly half of it: the half facing the Sun.

**We see that lit half from a changing angle.** As the Moon goes round the Earth, about once every 29½ days from one new Moon to the next, we see more or less of its lit half.

![The Moon's orbit seen from far above the Earth's North Pole, with sunlight coming from the left.](figures/half-moon.svg "The Moon's orbit seen from far above the Earth's North Pole, not to scale. The Sun, far off to the left, always lights the half of the Moon facing it; the Moon moves anticlockwise. What changes is how much of the lit half faces the Earth.")

- **New Moon.** The Moon is roughly between the Earth and the Sun. Its lit side faces away from us, and we see almost nothing.
- **Crescent, quarter and gibbous.** We see a growing share of the lit half: less than half at crescent, from a thin sliver upwards, exactly half the disc at first quarter, and most of it at gibbous.
- **Full Moon.** The Moon is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun, and we see the whole lit half.

**Why it can't be the shadow.** A crescent Moon is always seen fairly close to the Sun in the sky, just after sunset or before sunrise. The Earth is then nowhere near the line between the Sun and the Moon, so its shadow can't be falling on the Moon. The shape is wrong too: the two horns of a crescent always sit at opposite ends of a diameter of the Moon. The Earth's shadow, a circle nearly three times as wide as the Moon, takes a curved bite whose horns are usually much closer together, as photos of a partial lunar eclipse show.

## In the classroom

- **Lamp and ball.** Put a single bright lamp at one side of a darkened room. Each student holds a pale ball at arm's length, a little above head height so that their head doesn't block the light at full Moon, and turns slowly on the spot. The phases appear in order. Lower the ball into the shadow of the head at full Moon, and you have a lunar eclipse; that it needs an exact line-up is why eclipses are rarer than full Moons.
- **Look at the sky.** A crescent Moon near the setting Sun is the most persuasive evidence of all. Point out where the Sun is, and ask where the Earth's shadow must be pointing.
- **The New Zealand twist.** From the southern hemisphere, the Moon appears the other way up compared with northern hemisphere pictures. Here a waxing Moon is lit on its left side, so imported diagrams and rhymes for telling waxing from waning give the wrong answer.
- **Where it fits.** Earth and space in Years 7 to 10, and senior astronomy.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Earthshine.** A few days after new Moon, you can often see the dark part of the crescent glowing faintly grey. It is lit by sunlight reflected from the Earth. The old phrase for it is "the old Moon in the new Moon's arms".
- **The daytime Moon.** Many people are surprised to see the Moon in a blue daytime sky. Around first and last quarter, the Moon is above the horizon for about half the daylight hours, well away from the Sun.
- **Emoji and calendars.** Moon phase symbols are almost always drawn the northern hemisphere way round.

## Going deeper

**Why eclipses are rare.** If the Moon orbited in exactly the same plane as the Earth goes round the Sun, there would be a lunar eclipse every full Moon and a solar eclipse every new Moon. The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5°, so at most full Moons it passes above or below the Earth's shadow.

**The red Moon.** During a total lunar eclipse the Moon usually doesn't vanish: it turns a dull, coppery red. It is lit by sunlight bent through the Earth's atmosphere, light that has had most of its blue scattered away on the journey: the light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at that moment.

**Galileo and Venus.** In 1610 Galileo saw through his telescope that Venus shows a full set of phases, like the Moon. In Ptolemy's Earth-centred model, Venus always stays between the Earth and the Sun, so it could only ever appear as a crescent, never full. Its phases showed that Venus goes round the Sun. They ruled out Ptolemy's model, though not Tycho Brahe's compromise, in which the planets circle the Sun while the Sun circles the Earth.

**Two kinds of month.** The Moon takes about 27.3 days to go once round the Earth relative to the stars, but about 29.5 days to go from one new Moon to the next. In the meantime the Earth has moved along its orbit, so the Moon has to go a little further round to line up with the Sun again.

**Earth phases.** Seen from the Moon, the Earth shows phases too, opposite to the Moon's: at new Moon, the Earth would look full from the Moon. That nearly full Earth, seen from a crescent Moon a few days later, is what lights the earthshine.
