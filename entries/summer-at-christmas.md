## Question

In New Zealand, Christmas falls in summer. What is the main reason it is summer here in December?

## Option (correct)

Our hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, so sunlight hits it more directly.

> Right. In December the Sun climbs high in our sky, so each square metre of ground receives more sunlight. The tilt has a second effect too: the Sun stays up for longer each day. Both effects come from the tilt of the Earth's axis.

## Option

Our hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, so it is nearer to the Sun.

> The tilt is the right idea, but not because of distance. Tilting brings the southern hemisphere closer to the Sun by at most a few thousand kilometres, out of 150 million: a few thousandths of one percent, far too little to matter. What the tilt changes is the angle of the sunlight and the length of the day.

## Option

The Earth's orbit brings it closer to the Sun in December than in June.

> It is, as it happens: the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. But at that very moment it is winter in Canada and Europe, and both hemispheres are almost exactly the same distance from the Sun. Distance can't make it summer in one hemisphere and winter in the other. In any case, the Earth's distance from the Sun changes by only about 3% over the year.

## Option

The Sun gives out more energy in December than it does in June.

> The Sun's output is remarkably steady: it varies by only about a tenth of a percent, over an eleven-year cycle that has nothing to do with our calendar. And the same Sun shines on both hemispheres at once, yet it is winter in Canada while it is summer here.

## Why it's tempting

The closer you are to a fire, the warmer you feel. It seems natural that the same should hold for the Sun.

The explanation also starts from something true. The Earth's orbit really is an ellipse, not a perfect circle. Diagrams often draw it as a strongly squashed oval, seen at a slant, which makes the distance look as if it changes a great deal.

In New Zealand there is an extra trap: we are closest to the Sun during our summer, so the distance explanation seems to fit. In the northern hemisphere the timing is the wrong way round, which makes the misconception harder to sustain there, yet it is still very common.

Even people who know about the tilt often explain it with distance: "the southern hemisphere is leaning towards the Sun, so it's closer". The tilt is right, the mechanism is not.

## A better picture

**The orbit is almost a circle.** The Earth is about 147 million km from the Sun in early January and about 152 million km in early July, a difference of about 3%. Sunlight intensity goes as the inverse square of distance, so it varies by about 7% over the year. That is a real effect, but a small one, and it is the same for both hemispheres at once.

**The cause is the tilt.** The Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4° from upright, relative to the plane of its orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space all year. For half the year the southern hemisphere is tipped towards the Sun, and for the other half the northern hemisphere is. That has two effects.

- **Angle.** When the Sun is high, a beam of sunlight falls on a small patch of ground; when it is low, the same beam spreads over a larger area, so each square metre receives less. At Auckland, the midday Sun stands about 77° above the horizon at the December solstice and only about 30° at the June solstice. At midday, flat ground receives about twice as much sunlight per square metre in midsummer as in midwinter.
- **Day length.** In summer the Sun is also up for longer: about 14 hours 45 minutes from sunrise to sunset at midsummer in Auckland, against about 9 hours 40 minutes at midwinter.

## In the classroom

- **The two-hemisphere question.** Ask why Christmas is in summer in New Zealand but in winter in Canada. It takes the distance explanation apart in one step.
- **Torch and graph paper.** Shine a torch straight down onto graph paper and count the squares it lights. Then tilt the paper and count again. Same torch, same energy, more squares.
- **The tilted globe.** Carry a tilted globe round a lamp, keeping the axis pointing at the same wall throughout. Each hemisphere takes its turn leaning towards the light.
- **Draw the orbit to scale.** Drawn with an average radius of 10 cm, the Earth's orbit differs from a perfect circle by about a hundredth of a millimetre. The Sun sits only 1.7 mm from the centre.
- **Where it fits.** Earth and space in Years 7 to 10, and again in senior astronomy.

## Spotted in the wild

- **A Private Universe.** In a well known 1987 documentary, graduates, alumni and staff at a Harvard graduation ceremony were asked why we have seasons. Only 2 of the 23 gave a correct explanation, and the most common wrong answer, given confidently, was the distance explanation.
- **Christmas cards.** Many Christmas cards sold in New Zealand still show snow, robins and roaring fires, a reminder that the imagery comes from the north.
- **Diagrams.** Look out for orbit diagrams in books and on websites that show the Earth's orbit as a long, thin oval. Drawn at a slant for perspective, they quietly support the distance idea.

## Going deeper

**The distance effect is real.** At the top of the atmosphere, the whole Earth receives about 7% more sunlight in early January than in early July. Southern summers therefore get slightly stronger sunshine than northern summers, but they are also about a week shorter (see below), and the two effects cancel exactly: over the whole half year, each hemisphere receives the same total amount of sunlight. The southern hemisphere's large area of ocean, which warms and cools slowly, also evens out its seasons.

**Unequal seasons.** Because the Earth moves faster when it is nearer the Sun (Kepler's second law), the half of the year from the March equinox to the September equinox is about a week longer than the other half. Northern spring and summer last about 186 days; southern spring and summer about 179.

**Slow changes.** Over tens to hundreds of thousands of years, the tilt of the Earth's axis varies between about 22° and 24.5°, the shape of the orbit changes, and the date of closest approach drifts through the calendar. These Milankovitch cycles change how sunlight is shared between seasons and latitudes, and they are linked to the timing of the ice ages.

**Other worlds.** Uranus is tilted by about 98°, so it rolls round the Sun on its side, and each pole has decades of continuous daylight followed by decades of night. Mars, whose orbit is much more elongated than the Earth's, is a planet where distance matters more: its southern summers, near closest approach, are shorter and hotter than its northern ones.
