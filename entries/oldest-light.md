## Question

The cosmic microwave background, the oldest light we can see, has a redshift of about 1100. Put into the Doppler formula $v = cz$, that gives a speed of about 1100 times the speed of light.

What is the best explanation of this result?

## Option

Its source really is moving through space at 1100 times the speed of light.

> Nothing moves through space faster than light relative to its own surroundings. The redshift of very distant light doesn't measure a speed through space at all: it measures how much the universe has expanded while the light was travelling.

## Option

The measured redshift must be wrong, since nothing can go faster than light.

> The measurement is sound: the cosmic microwave background's redshift is known very precisely. What fails is the simple formula $v = cz$, which works only for small redshifts, from relatively nearby galaxies.

## Option

The relativistic Doppler formula is needed, which gives a speed just below light.

> A tempting fix, but on its own it misleads. The number just below the speed of light can be given a precise meaning, but it isn't the rate at which the source's distance from us grows, which can exceed the speed of light. For distant light, the redshift is best understood as recording how much the universe expanded while the light travelled, not a speed through space.

## Option (correct)

Its wavelength was stretched about 1100-fold as the universe expanded.

> Right. As light crosses an expanding universe, its wavelength stretches in step with the growing distances between galaxies. A redshift of 1100 means that wavelengths, and the distances across the universe, have grown about 1100-fold since the light set out.

## Why it's tempting

Redshift is usually taught straight after the Doppler effect for sound, using the familiar example of a passing siren dropping in pitch, so the two seem to be the same thing.

And for nearby galaxies, the Doppler formula $v \approx cz$ works very well. It is how Hubble's law was discovered, and how the speeds of nearby galaxies are still quoted. The point where it stops working is rarely flagged.

## A better picture

**Nearby, Doppler is fine.** For galaxies within a few hundred million light-years, the redshift $z$ is small and $v \approx cz$ is an excellent approximation, in the same way that Newton's laws are an excellent approximation at everyday speeds.

**Far away, stretching.** As light travels across an expanding universe, its wavelength is stretched in step with the growing distances between galaxies. If those distances have doubled since the light set out, its wavelength has doubled. In terms of the scale factor $a$, which measures how far apart galaxies are compared with today:

$$1 + z = \frac{\lambda_\text{observed}}{\lambda_\text{emitted}} = \frac{a_\text{now}}{a_\text{then}}$$

The redshift records how much the universe has expanded, not the speed of the source at any one moment.

**Faster than light, legitimately.** By Hubble's law, galaxies now more than about 14 billion light-years away have distances to us that are growing faster than light. Special relativity forbids anything from moving through space faster than light past its own neighbourhood; it doesn't forbid the distance between two far-apart galaxies from growing faster than light.

**An honest caveat.** Cosmologists debate how best to describe cosmological redshift. It can be treated, rigorously, as the build-up of many tiny Doppler shifts between neighbouring observers along the light's path, and the predictions are the same. What is wrong is using the simple formula $v = cz$ for distant galaxies, or picturing them as objects flying through a fixed space.

## In the classroom

- **The rubber band.** Draw a wave along a wide rubber band, then stretch the band. The wavelength grows with it: the stretching picture in one move.
- **Use a big redshift.** Ask students to work out $v = cz$ for the cosmic microwave background. The absurd answer invites the better explanation.
- **Label the approximation.** Teach $v \approx cz$ as the small-redshift form, and say where it fails.
- **Where it fits.** Cosmology in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Record-breaking galaxies.** The James Webb Space Telescope has found galaxies with redshifts above 14. Taking $v = cz$ literally would put them at 14 times the speed of light.
- **Headlines.** Stories about galaxies "racing away faster than light" encourage the picture of galaxies flying through space.
- **Genuine Doppler.** Police radar speed guns and weather radar use the genuine Doppler effect, from motion through space, which is exactly what makes the comparison so tempting.

## Going deeper

**Slow-motion explosions.** Distant supernovae appear to brighten and fade more slowly than nearby ones, by a factor of $1 + z$: a supernova at a redshift of 1 seems to unfold at half speed. This time dilation of distant events is exactly what the expansion predicts, and it has been measured.

**The cosmic microwave background.** The background radiation was released when the universe was about 380,000 years old and about 3,000 K. Since then the universe has expanded about 1100-fold, stretching the light's wavelengths and cooling it to the 2.7 K we measure today.
