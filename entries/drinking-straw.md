## Question

When you drink through a straw, the drink rises up the straw and into your mouth.

What makes the drink go up the straw?

## Option

Your mouth makes a vacuum, and the vacuum pulls the drink up.

> This is how it feels, and how we talk about it. But a region of low pressure just has less air in it, pushing less hard; air can push, but it can't pull. What you do is lower the air pressure in your mouth. The air outside, still at full pressure, pushes down on the drink in the glass and drives it up the straw.

## Option (correct)

The air pressing down on the drink in the glass pushes it up.

> Right. Sucking lowers the air pressure inside your mouth and the straw. The atmosphere, pressing down on the surface of the drink with a force of about 10 newtons on every square centimetre, pushes it up the straw towards the lower pressure.

## Option

The narrow straw draws the drink up by capillary action, like a wick.

> Capillary action does lift water up very narrow tubes, but in a drinking straw the effect is tiny: a few millimetres at most. And it can't explain why the drink rises only while you suck, and falls back when you stop.

## Why it's tempting

The words do a lot of the work. "Suck" and "suction" both describe pulling, and we use them for straws, vacuum cleaners and suction cups alike.

The sensation backs it up. When you suck on a straw you feel effort in your mouth and cheeks, as though you were pulling.

And we don't notice the air around us pressing on everything. The atmosphere pushes on every surface with great force, but it pushes equally from all sides, including from inside our bodies, so we never feel it. Only when the pressure on one side is lowered does its push become visible.

## A better picture

**The air is heavy.** The atmosphere above us has weight: there are roughly 10 tonnes of air above every square metre of the Earth's surface at sea level. That weight presses on everything, giving an air pressure of about 100,000 pascals, or about 10 newtons on every square centimetre.

**Pressure differences push.** Air pushes from where the pressure is higher towards where it is lower. When you suck on a straw, you lower the pressure in your mouth. The full pressure of the atmosphere on the surface of the drink is now greater than the pressure at the top of the straw, so the drink is pushed up.

**A vacuum can't pull.** A perfect vacuum exerts no pressure: with no particles in it, there is nothing to push or pull. All it can do is fail to push back.

**There's a limit.** Even with a perfect vacuum at the top of a straw, the atmosphere could push water up only about 10 metres: the height of a column of water whose weight matches the push of the air. Beyond that, no amount of sucking helps.

## In the classroom

- **Card over a glass.** Fill a glass to the brim with water, slide a card over the top, and turn it upside down over a sink. The card stays put: the air pushing up on the card holds the water in.
- **Collapsing can.** Boil a little water in an empty drink can, then use tongs to turn it upside down into a bowl of cold water. The steam condenses, the pressure inside drops, and the atmosphere crushes the can with a bang. (Eye protection, and the teacher handles the hot can.)
- **Two suction cups.** Press two suction cups or sink plungers together and have students try to pull them apart. Then ask what is holding them together.
- **Language.** Replace "the vacuum sucks" with "the air pushes in". It is awkward at first, but it makes the physics visible.
- **Where it fits.** Air pressure in Years 7 to 10; pressure in fluids in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Vacuum cleaners.** A fan inside lowers the air pressure in the machine, and the air of the room rushes in towards the low pressure, carrying the dust with it.
- **Suction cups.** A suction cup isn't sucked onto a window. Pressing it flat pushes out most of the air; as the rubber tries to spring back, the little air left beneath it spreads out and its pressure drops, so the atmosphere pushes the cup on. In a vacuum, outside a spacecraft, a suction cup wouldn't hold.
- **On the Moon.** A drinking straw wouldn't work out on the Moon's surface, where there is no atmosphere to push the drink up. (An open drink there would boil furiously, cooling itself until what was left froze, but that's another story.)

## Going deeper

**The pump makers' puzzle.** In the 17th century, pump makers knew that suction pumps couldn't lift water more than about 10 metres. Galileo discussed the puzzle, putting the limit down to the limited strength of the vacuum's pull. In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli, who had briefly been Galileo's assistant, explained it: the water was being pushed up by the weight of the atmosphere, which could support only so tall a column. He used mercury, 13.6 times denser than water, to make a much shorter column, about 76 cm tall, and so invented the barometer.

**Weighing the air.** In 1648, Blaise Pascal had his brother-in-law, Florin Périer, carry a mercury barometer up the Puy de Dôme, a mountain in France. The mercury column was several centimetres shorter at the top, where there was less air above it pressing down.

**Horses against the air.** In the 1650s (the famous demonstration is usually dated to 1654), Otto von Guericke fitted together two copper hemispheres about half a metre across and pumped out the air between them. Teams of horses pulling in opposite directions couldn't separate them, though when air was let back in, they fell apart.

**How trees beat the limit.** The tallest trees lift water over 100 metres, ten times higher than any suction pump could. They don't use suction: water evaporating from the leaves pulls on unbroken columns of water in the trunk, and the water molecules cling together strongly enough to be pulled up under tension. That is a genuine pull, but by the water itself, not by a vacuum.
