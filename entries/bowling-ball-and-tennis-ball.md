## Question

You hold a bowling ball in one hand and a tennis ball in the other, both 2 metres above the floor, and let go of them at the same moment.

Which statement best describes what happens, and why?

## Option

The bowling ball lands clearly first, because gravity pulls harder on it.

> This was Aristotle's view, and it feels obvious. Gravity really does pull harder on the bowling ball: roughly a hundred times harder. But the bowling ball is also roughly a hundred times harder to speed up. The two effects cancel exactly, and both balls gain speed at the same rate.

## Option (correct)

They land together, because a bigger pull is needed to speed up a bigger mass.

> Right, and for the right reason. The pull of gravity is proportional to mass, and so is the resistance to speeding up, so every object falls with the same acceleration, about 9.8 m/s². Strictly, air resistance holds the tennis ball back slightly: when the bowling ball lands, the tennis ball is still about 2 to 3 cm above the floor, roughly four thousandths of a second behind, far too close to tell apart by eye.

## Option

They land together, because gravity pulls on both with the same force.

> The right result, for a reason that doesn't hold. Gravity pulls on the bowling ball roughly a hundred times harder than on the tennis ball: that is what its greater weight means. They land together because the bowling ball is also a hundred times harder to speed up.

## Option

The tennis ball lands first, because heavy things are harder to get moving.

> Half of the right idea. A heavy object is harder to speed up, but gravity also pulls on it harder, in exactly the same proportion. The two effects cancel.

## Why it's tempting

Everyday experience seems to confirm it. A stone falls faster than a feather, and a book faster than a sheet of paper. The real cause, air resistance, is invisible, and it affects light, spread-out objects far more than heavy, compact ones. So in daily life, heavier things often do reach the ground first.

Weight is also something we feel directly. A bowling ball pulls down on your hand much harder than a tennis ball does, so it seems obvious that it will rush downwards faster. That part is true: the pull on it really is larger. What intuition leaves out is the other half of the story, that a more massive object is harder to speed up.

The idea has a long pedigree. Aristotle taught that heavier bodies fall faster, and his view remained the dominant one for nearly two thousand years, although John Philoponus challenged it with dropping experiments as early as the 6th century.

## A better picture

Two things decide how quickly an object speeds up: how hard it is pushed or pulled, and how much mass it has.

- **The pull.** The force of gravity on an object, its weight, is proportional to its mass: $W = mg$. A bowling ball of mass 6 kg is pulled about a hundred times harder than a 58 g tennis ball.
- **The response.** The acceleration produced by a force is the force divided by the mass, $a = F/m$. The bowling ball needs about a hundred times more force for the same acceleration.

Put them together and the mass cancels:

$$a = \frac{W}{m} = \frac{mg}{m} = g$$

Every object falls with the same acceleration, about 9.8 m/s² near the Earth's surface, whatever its mass.

A useful picture: one person pushing one supermarket trolley, and ten people pushing a line of ten trolleys. Ten times the push and ten times the mass give exactly the same speeding up.

**Where air comes in.** Air resistance depends on an object's size, shape and speed, not its mass. The same drag is a big fraction of a light object's weight but a tiny fraction of a heavy one's. So in air, of two objects with the same size and shape, the heavier one does fall faster, and the difference grows on long drops. The intuition isn't wrong about the world; it is wrong about the reason.

## In the classroom

- **Paper and book.** Drop a sheet of paper and a book side by side: the book wins easily. Now lay the paper flat on top of the book (it must be smaller than the book) and drop them together: the paper stays on the book all the way down. Finally, crumple the paper into a tight ball and race it against the book again.
- **Galileo's argument.** Suppose heavy things fall faster. Tie a heavy ball to a light one. The light ball should hold the heavy one back, so the pair falls more slowly than the heavy ball alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy ball, so it should fall faster. Both can't be true. Students enjoy finding the contradiction for themselves.
- **Watch it in a vacuum.** Show the footage from Apollo 15 or from the vacuum chamber described below. Ask for predictions first.
- **Language.** "Heavier objects are pulled harder, and are harder to speed up" keeps both halves of the story in view.
- **Where it fits.** Falling and forces in Years 9 and 10; Newton's second law in Year 11 and beyond.

## Spotted in the wild

- **On the Moon.** On 2 August 1971, at the end of the Apollo 15 moonwalks, astronaut David Scott dropped a geologist's hammer and a falcon feather together in front of the television camera. With no air, they hit the ground at the same moment. "How about that!" he said. "Which proves that Mr. Galileo was correct in his findings."
- **In the world's largest vacuum chamber.** For the 2014 BBC series *Human Universe*, Brian Cox watched a bowling ball and a bundle of feathers dropped together in NASA's Space Power Facility in Ohio, first in air and then with the air pumped out. In the vacuum, they fell side by side.
- **Skydiving.** Here the intuition is right, because of air. A heavier skydiver falls faster than a lighter one in the same position, and two people strapped together for a tandem jump would fall uncomfortably fast, so tandem rigs deploy a small drogue parachute to slow the pair to a normal freefall speed.

## Going deeper

**Galileo and the tower.** The famous story of Galileo dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa comes from his student and biographer Vincenzo Viviani, writing more than sixty years later, and historians doubt it happened as told. Galileo's lasting case was made with careful experiments on balls rolling down slopes, and with the thought experiment above, published in his *Two New Sciences* (1638). A drop experiment was in fact published earlier, by the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin, who in 1586 reported dropping two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, about 30 feet from a church tower in Delft: they landed so close together that the two impacts sounded as one.

**Two kinds of mass.** The cancellation above uses mass twice: once as the thing gravity pulls on (gravitational mass), and once as the resistance to being accelerated (inertial mass). Nothing in Newton's theory says these must be in the same proportion for every material; it is an experimental fact. Newton tested it with pendulums of different materials, and Loránd Eötvös, around 1900, with a torsion balance. In 2022 the MICROSCOPE satellite reported that titanium and platinum fall alike to within about one part in $10^{15}$.

**Einstein's starting point.** Einstein took this equality as a fundamental principle, the equivalence principle: if everything falls the same way, then falling freely is indistinguishable, in a small enough region, from floating in space with no gravity at all. That idea became the foundation of general relativity, in which gravity is no longer a force but the curvature of spacetime.
