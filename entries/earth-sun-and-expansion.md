## Question

The universe is expanding: distant galaxies are getting further apart from each other.

What is this expansion doing to the distance between the Earth and the Sun?

## Option

Increasing it in step with the universe, since space itself is stretching.

> If space stretched everything evenly, the Solar System would grow in step with the universe. But the Solar System is held together by gravity, which keeps the Earth's orbit the size it is. The expansion only shows up between galaxies too far apart for gravity to hold them together.

## Option

Increasing it very slowly, since gravity only partly resists the stretching.

> A natural compromise: the expansion is weak, so perhaps it stretches the Solar System just a little. But it doesn't gradually stretch systems held together by gravity at all; gravity keeps the Earth's orbit its own size. (The Earth's distance from the Sun does change very slightly over time, by about 1.5 cm a year, but for another reason: the Sun is slowly losing mass.)

## Option (correct)

Nothing measurable, since gravity holds the Solar System together.

> Right. The expansion of the universe shows up only between galaxies, or groups of galaxies, that are too far apart for gravity to hold them together. The Solar System is held together by the Sun's gravity, which keeps the Earth's orbit the size it is.

## Option

Nothing we could notice, since the Earth, the Sun and our rulers all grow in proportion.

> If everything, including our rulers and our atoms, grew in proportion, no measurement could ever detect the expansion. We can detect it precisely because galaxies, planets and measuring instruments keep their sizes while the gaps between distant galaxies grow.

## Why it's tempting

Cosmologists often say that "space itself is expanding". Pictured as a stretching of space, it seems to follow that every piece of space must stretch, and everything in it along with it.

The balloon analogy, in which dots drawn on a balloon spread apart as it is blown up, makes it worse: the dots themselves stretch along with the rubber.

And "the universe is expanding" sounds as if it applies to everything in it. It is natural to assume that includes the Earth, the Solar System and ourselves.

## A better picture

**Bound systems stay bound.** The expansion is a property of the universe on the largest scales, where matter is spread fairly evenly. Where matter is gathered into something held together by a force, that force wins completely.

- **Atoms and molecules** are held together by electric forces, vastly stronger than any effect of the expansion.
- **The Earth, the Solar System and the Milky Way** are held together by gravity. The expansion isn't making the Earth's orbit bigger.
- **Groups of galaxies** are also held together by gravity. The Milky Way and the Andromeda galaxy are approaching each other at about 110 km/s.

**Where the expansion shows.** Only between galaxies, or groups of galaxies, far enough apart that their gravity can't hold them together, do the distances grow. That is why Hubble's law is a law about distant galaxies.

**The ruler argument.** If everything, including our rulers, grew in proportion, the expansion would be undetectable. We measure it precisely because some things don't expand.

## In the classroom

- **Raisins don't swell.** In the raisin-bread analogy, the raisins themselves don't grow as the dough rises; only the dough between them stretches. The raisins stand for galaxies and anything else held together.
- **Stickers on a balloon.** Instead of drawing dots on a balloon, stick on small paper discs. As the balloon inflates, the discs keep their size while the distances between them grow.
- **The Andromeda question.** Show that our nearest large galactic neighbour is coming towards us, and ask how that fits with an expanding universe.
- **Where it fits.** The universe in Years 9 and 10; cosmology in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Andromeda's light.** Most galaxies' light is shifted towards the red, because the distances to them are growing. Andromeda's light is shifted towards the blue. Part of this is because the Sun's orbit around the Milky Way carries us towards it, but about 110 km/s is a genuine approach: the two galaxies' mutual gravity has overcome the expansion between them.
- **Headlines.** Phrases such as "the fabric of space is stretching" encourage the picture of everything being pulled apart.
- **Diagrams.** Pictures of the expanding universe often show galaxies themselves growing larger as time goes on.

## Going deeper

**Dark energy.** Since the late 1990s, observations have shown that the expansion of the universe is speeding up, driven by what is called dark energy. Even so, its effect inside a galaxy, let alone the Solar System, is utterly negligible compared with the gravity holding them together.

**Will the Milky Way and Andromeda collide?** The two galaxies have long been expected to merge in several billion years. A 2025 study that included the pull of the Triangulum galaxy and the Large Magellanic Cloud put the odds of a merger within ten billion years at only about even. A 2026 reanalysis using improved measurements of Andromeda's sideways motion raised them to about 90 percent. The answer depends on that sideways motion, which is still hard to measure.

**What would it take?** Some speculative models of dark energy have it growing stronger over time, eventually tearing apart even galaxies, planets and atoms, a scenario called the "Big Rip". Current observations don't require this, and most cosmologists don't expect it.
