## Question

Stand in front of a bathroom mirror and raise your right hand. Your reflection seems to raise its left hand. Yet your reflection's head is still at the top and its feet at the bottom.

Why does a mirror swap left and right, but not top and bottom?

## Option (correct)

Because we picture ourselves turning round to stand where the reflection is.

> Right. A mirror reverses only one direction, the one at right angles to its surface: point at the mirror, and your reflection points straight back at you. Directions along the mirror stay as they are. Your hand on the window side of the room is reflected on the window side, and your head at the top is reflected at the top. Left and right only seem to swap because, to compare yourself with the reflection, you imagine turning round about a vertical axis to stand in its place, and that imagined turn is what swaps them.

## Option

Because our two eyes are side by side, not one above the other.

> A popular explanation, but it can't be right. Close one eye, and the mirror behaves in exactly the same way. A camera with a single lens photographs the same reflection. Nothing about the image depends on how many eyes you look with.

## Option

Because a mirror reverses light from side to side, but not from top to bottom.

> This is the idea the question invites, but it isn't what a mirror does. Point to the left, along the mirror, and your reflection points to the same side of the room; point up, and it points up. The only direction a mirror reverses is the one at right angles to its surface: point at the mirror, and your reflection points back at you.

## Why it's tempting

The effect is real and striking. Wave your right hand and the figure in the mirror waves what looks like its left. Writing in a mirror reads backwards. The asymmetry seems to demand an explanation, and the puzzle of "why one direction and not the other" feels deep.

There is a genuine truth underneath: a mirror really does turn a right hand into a left hand. The reflection of a right glove is shaped like a left glove. So something is reversed. The mistake is in naming which direction.

We compare ourselves with our reflection by imagining ourselves in its place, which means imagining turning round to face back the way we came. That imagined turn is so automatic that we don't notice we have made it.

## A better picture

**A mirror reverses one direction: the one at right angles to its surface.** A point in front of the mirror has its image the same distance behind it. Directions along the mirror, left–right and up–down, are left exactly as they are.

**Try it.** Point your finger to the left, along the mirror: the reflection's finger points to the same side of the room. Point up: the reflection points up. Point at the mirror: the reflection points back at you. Only the front–back direction has flipped.

**So where does left–right come from?** To compare yourself with your reflection, you imagine walking round to stand in its place, turning to face the other way. That turn, about a vertical axis, is what swaps your left and right. The mirror didn't do it; you did, in your head. We turn round a vertical axis because that is how people turn.

**Handedness.** Reversing a single direction turns a right-handed object into a left-handed one. No rotation can do that: you can't turn a right glove into a left glove by rotating it. That is the real sense in which a mirror image is "reversed".

## In the classroom

- **Point at the mirror.** Have students point in different directions in front of a mirror and say which way the reflection points. Only pointing at the mirror gives a reversal.
- **Mirror writing, two ways.** Write a word on a card and hold it up to a mirror by turning the card round about a vertical axis: the word reads backwards. Now turn the card round about a horizontal axis instead, top over bottom: the letters still run left to right, but each one is flipped top to bottom, like writing reflected in a lake. The mirror hasn't changed; the way the card was turned has.
- **Two mirrors.** Stand two flat mirrors at right angles, like a half-open book, and look into the corner. You see yourself as others see you: raise your right hand, and the reflection raises its right hand, the one opposite yours, just as a friend facing you would.
- **Where it fits.** Reflection in Years 7 to 10; symmetry and handedness in senior physics and chemistry.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Ambulances.** The word AMBULANCE is often written in mirror writing on the front of the vehicle, so that drivers ahead read it the right way round in their rear-view mirrors.
- **Selfies.** Phone cameras usually show the front camera's preview like a mirror, but many save the photo unflipped. In a classic 1977 study, people preferred their own mirror image, the face they are used to seeing, while their friends preferred the true photo.
- **Leonardo's notebooks.** Leonardo da Vinci wrote most of his notes in mirror script, from right to left, which can be read easily in a mirror.

## Going deeper

**Two reflections make a rotation.** Reflect something in one mirror and then in another, and the result is equivalent to a rotation: the handedness is reversed twice, so it ends up as it started. That is why the corner of two mirrors at right angles gives an image that isn't reversed.

**Handed molecules.** Many molecules exist in two forms that are mirror images of each other, like a pair of hands. They can behave differently in the body. Carvone is a famous example: one form smells of spearmint and its mirror image of caraway seeds.

**Is nature mirror-symmetric?** For a long time physicists assumed that the mirror image of any physical process is also a possible process. In 1956 Chien-Shiung Wu and her colleagues showed that this fails for the weak nuclear force: in the radioactive decay of cobalt-60 nuclei lined up in a magnetic field, electrons came out mostly in the direction opposite to the nuclear spins. In the mirror image of that process, the spins are reversed but the electron directions are not, so the electrons would favour the spin direction. Nature never does that.
