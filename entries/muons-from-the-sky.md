## Question

Muons are created when cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere, typically about 15 km up, and most of those that reach the ground travel at more than 99.5% of the speed of light. A muon at rest lasts on average only about 2.2 microseconds before it decays.

Yet large numbers of these muons reach the ground. How?

## Option (correct)

Measured from the ground, their internal clocks run slow, so they last longer.

> Right. At these speeds, measured from the ground, a muon's internal clock runs slow by a factor of several tens, so on average it lasts well over 50 microseconds, long enough for a large fraction to cover 15 km. Without time dilation, only about one in ten billion would survive the trip. (From the muon's own point of view, its clock runs normally, but the atmosphere is contracted to a small fraction of its thickness.)

## Option

They only appear to last longer, because of the time light takes to reach us.

> Light-travel delays can change what we see, but they can't change how many muons arrive at a detector. The muons are counted on the ground, and far more arrive than could if their lifetimes there were 2.2 microseconds. The slowing of their clocks is real, not an appearance.

## Option

They travel faster than light, so they cover the distance in time.

> Nothing with mass can reach the speed of light, let alone exceed it, and measurements confirm that muons travel slightly slower than light. Even at the speed of light, a muon lasting 2.2 microseconds would cover only about 660 m.

## Option

2.2 microseconds is only an average, and the ones that live much longer are the ones that arrive.

> Some muons do outlive the average, but very few by enough. The trip takes about 50 microseconds, more than 20 average lifetimes, and the chance of a muon lasting that long, if its lifetime were really 2.2 microseconds, is about one in ten billion. Far more than that arrive.

## Why it's tempting

It seems too strange to be real. A clock is a clock; why should its ticking depend on who is watching?

The language of relativity encourages the idea. "From your frame of reference" sounds like "from your point of view", and points of view feel like matters of opinion, not facts.

Some effects of motion change what we receive without changing the source. The Doppler effect changes the pitch of a passing siren, and light takes time to reach us from moving objects. Time dilation is easily lumped together with them.

And the symmetry is puzzling: each of two observers moving past each other measures the other's clock running slow. If both are "right", it seems neither can be real.

## A better picture

**A real, measured effect.** Two observers in relative motion measure different times between the same two events. This remains after every signal delay has been allowed for, so it isn't a matter of how things look. And when clocks are separated and brought back together, they really do show different elapsed times.

**The Lorentz factor.** A clock moving at speed $v$ is measured to run slow by the factor

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$$

which is very close to 1 at everyday speeds, but grows rapidly near the speed of light. At 99.5% of the speed of light, $\gamma \approx 10$; at 99.98%, it is about 50.

**The muons, in numbers.** The trip down through 15 km takes about 50 microseconds, as measured from the ground. With a lifetime of 2.2 microseconds, only about one muon in ten billion would survive that long. A typical muon reaching the ground has a Lorentz factor of several tens, stretching its lifetime to well over 50 microseconds, so a large fraction survive. (Real muons also lose energy, about 2 GeV, crossing the atmosphere; the simple numbers ignore this, but the conclusion is the same.)

**Separating the effects.** The classical Doppler shift and light-travel delays can be calculated and removed. What remains after removing them is time dilation, and it is physical.

## In the classroom

- **Work through the numbers.** Have students calculate how many muons should reach the ground with and without time dilation. The difference, a factor of billions, rules out "it's just how it looks".
- **Cloud chamber.** A diffusion cloud chamber shows the tracks of cosmic-ray muons passing through the classroom: long, straight, thin trails.
- **The twin paradox.** When a travelling twin returns, both twins stand in the same place at the same time and compare ages. There is no point of view from which the age difference is an illusion.
- **Where it fits.** Special relativity in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Muons in your body.** At sea level, roughly one cosmic-ray muon passes through each square centimetre of the ground every minute: many pass through you every second.
- **GPS.** Clocks on GPS satellites run slow because of their speed and fast because they are higher up in the Earth's gravity; together, they gain about 38 microseconds a day on clocks on the ground. GPS has to allow for this, or its ranging errors would grow by about 10 km a day.
- **Flying clocks.** In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew atomic clocks around the world on commercial flights, eastwards and westwards. When they returned, the clocks disagreed with clocks left behind, agreeing with relativity's predictions within the experimental uncertainty.

## Going deeper

**The first muon measurements.** In 1941 Bruno Rossi and David Hall compared the numbers of muons at different altitudes and found that the faster muons decayed more slowly, as relativity predicted. In 1962 and 1963 David Frisch and James Smith made a famous filmed measurement, counting muons on Mount Washington in New Hampshire and near sea level.

**Muons in a ring.** In a storage ring at CERN in the 1970s, muons circulating at nearly the speed of light, with $\gamma$ of about 29, were measured to live about 29 times longer than muons at rest, in agreement with relativity to about one part in a thousand.

**Time dilation at walking pace.** Modern optical atomic clocks are so precise that, in 2010, they detected time dilation at speeds of about 10 metres per second, and the gravitational difference in the rate of time between clocks 33 cm apart in height. By 2022 the gravitational effect had been resolved across a single millimetre-tall cloud of atoms.
