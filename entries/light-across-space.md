## Question

Sunlight crosses about 150 million km of almost empty space to reach the Earth. Light is a wave.

What is doing the waving as it crosses that space?

## Option (correct)

Electric and magnetic fields, which need no material to exist.

> Right. Light is a ripple in the electromagnetic field: changing electric and magnetic fields, linked so that each sustains the other, the pattern travelling at the speed of light. Fields exist in empty space, so light needs no material to travel through.

## Option

The thin gas between the planets, which is the only material out there.

> The gas between the planets is extraordinarily thin, only a handful of particles in each cubic centimetre, and light doesn't need it anyway: it crosses the even emptier space between galaxies, and passes through a vacuum chamber in a laboratory, without any trouble.

## Option

An invisible substance filling all of space, called the ether.

> This was exactly what physicists believed for most of the 19th century: light was thought to be a vibration of the "luminiferous ether". But every attempt to detect the ether failed, most famously the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887, and Einstein's relativity, in 1905, showed it wasn't needed. The ether is no longer part of physics.

## Option

Particles of light, photons, wiggling from side to side as they travel.

> The wave in light isn't anything moving sideways through space: it is the strength of the electric and magnetic fields that rises and falls, at each point along the way. Photons describe how light gives up its energy, in lumps, not a path that wiggles.

## Why it's tempting

The first waves anyone meets are waves in materials: ripples on water, waves on a rope, sound in air. In every one of them, something is obviously doing the waving, and without it there is no wave.

So the material comes to seem part of the definition: a wave is "a disturbance travelling through a medium". Applied to light, that definition demands a medium.

The idea of an oscillation with no material oscillating is genuinely hard to picture. Physicists themselves believed in the ether until the early 20th century.

## A better picture

**Two families of waves.** *Mechanical waves*, such as sound, water waves, seismic waves and waves on a string, are disturbances in a material, and genuinely need one. *Electromagnetic waves*, such as light, radio, microwaves and X-rays, are different.

**Ripples in fields.** An electromagnetic wave is a travelling pattern of changing electric and magnetic fields. The changing electric and magnetic fields are linked, each sustaining the other, and they travel together as the wave. Electric and magnetic fields exist in empty space, so nothing material is needed.

**Maxwell's discovery.** In the 1860s, James Clerk Maxwell found that his equations for electricity and magnetism predicted waves of changing fields, travelling at a speed he could calculate from measurements of electric and magnetic forces. It came out very close to the measured speed of light, and he concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. (Maxwell himself still pictured these waves as vibrations of an ether.) Between 1886 and 1888, Heinrich Hertz made and detected such waves in his laboratory: the first deliberate demonstration of radio waves.

## In the classroom

- **The bell jar.** An electric bell ringing inside a glass jar grows quieter as the air is pumped out, yet stays clearly visible the whole time. Sound needs the air; light doesn't. (Use a safety screen with any evacuated glass jar.)
- **A phone in a jar.** Put a mobile phone in the bell jar and call it as the air is pumped out. The ringtone fades, but the phone still receives the call: radio waves cross the vacuum. Use an old phone with vibration turned off, and keep the pumping time short, since phone batteries can swell at low pressure.
- **Sort them.** Give students a list (sound, light, radio, water waves, X-rays, earthquake waves, microwaves) and have them sort it into "needs a material" and "doesn't".
- **Where it fits.** Waves in Years 9 and 10; electromagnetic waves in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"In space, no one can hear you scream."** The tagline of the 1979 film *Alien* gets the physics exactly right, for sound. Plenty of other films fill space battles with roaring engines and booming explosions.
- **Talking to Voyager.** NASA still exchanges radio signals with the Voyager 1 spacecraft, nearly 26 billion km away, almost a light-day, across almost perfectly empty space.
- **Starlight.** Every other star you see sends its light across tens of trillions of kilometres of space with essentially nothing in it.

## Going deeper

**The speed of light from electricity and magnetism.** Maxwell's equations give the speed of electromagnetic waves as $c = 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}$, where $\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are constants measured in experiments on electric and magnetic forces. That this comes out as the speed of light was one of the great unifications in physics.

**The ether's demise.** If light travelled through an ether, the Earth's motion through it should have changed the measured speed of light in different directions. Michelson and Morley looked for that effect in 1887 and found none. Einstein's special relativity, in 1905, took the speed of light in a vacuum to be the same for every observer, built a consistent theory on that idea, and showed that no ether was needed.

**Ripples in spacetime.** Gravitational waves, first detected directly by the LIGO observatories in September 2015, are ripples in the shape of spacetime itself. They too need no material to travel through.
