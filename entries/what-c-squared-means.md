## Question

In Einstein's equation $E = mc^2$, what does $c^2$ represent?

## Option

The speed at which mass moves when it is turned into energy.

> Nothing in the equation describes anything moving. $c^2$ has units of m²/s², which aren't the units of a speed: they are joules per kilogram. It is the conversion factor between mass and energy.

## Option

A speed limit even faster than the speed of light itself.

> "The speed of light squared" sounds like an even more extreme speed, but squaring a speed doesn't give a speed: $c^2$ has units of m²/s², not m/s. Multiplied by a mass in kilograms, it gives an energy in joules.

## Option (correct)

The number of joules of energy that go with each kilogram of mass.

> Right. $c^2 \approx 9 \times 10^{16}$ m²/s², and m²/s² is the same as joules per kilogram. So $c^2$ converts a mass in kilograms into an energy in joules: every kilogram of mass corresponds to about $9 \times 10^{16}$ joules. Nothing is moving at $c^2$.

## Option

The kinetic energy of each kilogram of mass moving at the speed of light.

> A natural guess, since $c^2$ looks like the $v^2$ in kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. But nothing with mass can move at the speed of light, and the rest energy $mc^2$ belongs to an object that isn't moving at all. (Even in Newtonian physics, a kilogram moving at speed $c$ would have kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}c^2$ joules, not $c^2$.) $c^2$ is simply the conversion factor between mass and energy.

## Why it's tempting

The equation is nearly always introduced as "energy equals mass times the speed of light squared", and the squaring makes $c^2$ sound like an even more extreme version of the speed of light.

It also looks like kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, in which $v^2$ really is the square of a speed. It is natural to read $c^2$ the same way, as the square of how fast something is going.

## A better picture

**Look at the units.** The speed of light is $c \approx 3 \times 10^8$ m/s, so $c^2 \approx 9 \times 10^{16}$ m²/s². That isn't a speed, which would be in m/s. It is an energy per unit mass:

$$\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{s}^2} = \frac{\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}{\text{kg}} = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$$

**A conversion factor.** $c^2$ converts a mass in kilograms into the equivalent energy in joules, just as 1000 converts kilometres into metres. Multiplying a mass in kilograms by $c^2$ gives the corresponding energy in joules.

**Why it is so big.** Because $c^2$ is enormous, a small mass corresponds to a huge energy. One gram corresponds to $9 \times 10^{13}$ J, about the energy released by 21,000 tonnes of TNT. That is why nuclear reactions, which change the mass of their fuel by a fraction of a percent, release so much energy.

**Not only nuclear.** The same conversion applies to every change in energy: a hot cup of tea has slightly more mass than a cold one. The changes in everyday processes are just far too small to measure (see [[flask-of-tea]]).

## In the classroom

- **Unit drill.** Write the equation with units: $E\,[\text{J}] = m\,[\text{kg}] \times c^2\,[\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2]$, and check that the units match.
- **One gram.** Work out the energy of 1 g of mass, then ask: what is moving at $9 \times 10^{16}$ anything? Nothing.
- **Other conversion factors.** Compare $c^2$ with other conversion factors: 1000 for kilometres to metres, 3600 for hours to seconds. None of them is a speed.
- **Where it fits.** Mass-energy equivalence in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **T-shirts and logos.** $E = mc^2$ is probably the most famous equation in the world, and it is usually explained as "the speed of light squared", with no hint of what that means.
- **Popular science.** Descriptions of mass being "converted into energy at the speed of light squared" make it sound like a process happening at a speed.
- **The atomic bomb.** The Hiroshima bomb released its energy from a change in mass of less than a gram, a vivid illustration of how large $c^2$ is.

## Going deeper

**Einstein's question.** Einstein's 1905 paper establishing the general idea was titled "Does the inertia of a body depend upon its energy content?" He concluded that a body giving out energy $L$ loses mass $L/V^2$, using $V$ for the speed of light. The modern form $E = mc^2$ came later.

**Natural units.** Many physicists choose units in which $c = 1$, measuring distance in light-seconds and time in seconds, for example. Then $E = mc^2$ becomes simply $E = m$: mass and energy are measured in the same units. The $c^2$ was only ever there to convert between the units we happened to choose.

**The fuller equation.** For a moving object, $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$, where $p$ is its momentum. Here too, $c$ appears only to convert momentum and mass into the units of energy.
