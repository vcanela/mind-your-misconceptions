## Question

Suppose the Sun were a giant fire, getting as much energy from each kilogram of its mass as burning coal does.

At its present brightness, roughly how long could it keep shining?

## Option

A few hundred years

> Burning releases much less energy than nuclear fusion, but it isn't quite that feeble: a Sun releasing as much energy per kilogram as burning coal would last about 5,000 years.

## Option (correct)

A few thousand years

> Right. Burning coal releases about 30 million joules per kilogram. The Sun's mass, about $2 \times 10^{30}$ kg, would then hold about $6 \times 10^{37}$ J. Giving out about $3.8 \times 10^{26}$ W, it would run through that in about 5,000 years. The Earth is 4.5 billion years old, so the Sun can't be a fire.

## Option

A few million years

> Even a few million years is hundreds of times too long. Burning coal releases only about 30 million joules per kilogram, and the Sun gives out energy so fast that a coal-powered Sun would run out in about 5,000 years.

## Option

A few billion years

> A few billion years is about how long the Sun has actually been shining, which is exactly why it can't be a fire: chemical burning would run out about a million times sooner, in about 5,000 years. Only nuclear fusion releases enough energy per kilogram.

## Why it's tempting

The Sun is hot and bright and glows yellow and orange, like a flame. Fire is the only everyday source of heat and light on anything like that scale.

Language reinforces it: we talk about the "burning Sun" and the "blazing heat", and stars are often described as balls of fire.

Close-up pictures of the Sun help too. Solar prominences, huge loops of glowing gas leaping from its surface, look exactly like flames.

## A better picture

**Burning is chemistry.** A fire is a chemical reaction: fuel combines with oxygen, and rearranging the electrons in the molecules releases a small amount of energy for each molecule. The Sun contains far too little oxygen to burn its hydrogen, and it is too hot for molecules to survive in most of it.

**The numbers rule it out.** Even if the Sun could burn, chemistry releases far too little energy per kilogram. A Sun releasing as much energy per kilogram as burning coal would last only about 5,000 years.

**Fusion is nuclear.** In the Sun's core, at about 15 million °C, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium. A helium nucleus has slightly less mass than the four hydrogen nuclei that made it, and the missing 0.7% of the mass is released as energy, by $E = mc^2$. Per kilogram of fuel, that is millions of times more than any chemical reaction, enough to keep the Sun shining steadily, as it does now, for about ten billion years. It is roughly halfway through.

**It glows because it is hot.** The light we see comes from the Sun's visible surface, at about 5,500 °C, glowing the way any very hot object glows, not from flames.

## In the classroom

- **Do the coal calculation.** It takes three lines, and the result, about 5,000 years, is plainly impossible.
- **Glowing isn't burning.** A hot iron bar glows without burning, and so does a light bulb filament. Glowing means hot; it doesn't mean fire.
- **Tell the history.** The question of what powers the Sun was a genuine scientific puzzle for decades (see below), which makes a good story about evidence and missing ideas.
- **Where it fits.** The Sun and stars in Years 7 to 10; fusion and stellar physics in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"The burning Sun."** Everyday phrases, poems and songs describe the Sun as burning or ablaze.
- **What colour is the Sun?** The Sun is often drawn yellow or orange, like a flame. Seen from space, its light is essentially white; it looks yellow or orange to us partly because the atmosphere scatters away some of its blue light, most strongly near sunrise and sunset.
- **Neutrinos.** Fusion in the Sun's core produces vast numbers of ghostly particles called neutrinos, which stream straight out through the Sun. About 65 billion of them pass through each square centimetre of your body every second: direct evidence of the nuclear reactions in the core.

## Going deeper

**Kelvin's mistake.** In the 19th century, the best available explanation for the Sun's energy was that it was slowly shrinking, heating up as gravity squeezed it. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, calculated that this could keep the Sun shining for some tens of millions of years, at most about 100 million, and used the figure to argue that the Earth was much younger than geologists and Darwin needed. His calculation for a shrinking Sun was sound; what he couldn't know was that nuclear energy existed.

**Solving the puzzle.** In 1920 Arthur Eddington suggested that the Sun's energy came from turning hydrogen into helium. In 1938 Hans Bethe and Charles Critchfield worked out the proton–proton chain, the main route in the Sun. In 1939 Bethe worked out the second route, the CNO cycle, which Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker had found independently in 1938.

**Losing weight.** The Sun gives out energy so fast that its mass falls by over four million tonnes every second, by $E = mc^2$. It is so massive that, over its whole life burning hydrogen, it will lose only a small fraction of a percent of its mass this way.
