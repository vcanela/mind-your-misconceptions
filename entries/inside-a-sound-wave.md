## Question

Out in the open, a loudspeaker plays a steady note. Apart from the fast, random jiggling of its molecules, how does the air in front of the loudspeaker move as the sound passes through it?

## Option

Up and down, at right angles to the way the sound travels.

> This is the picture that the usual wavy-line diagram suggests. But that curve is a graph of pressure, or of how far the air has moved, against position: it isn't a picture of the air's motion. In air, the air moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels.

## Option (correct)

Back and forth, along the direction the sound travels.

> Right. The loudspeaker cone pushes and pulls on the air, making regions where the air is squashed together (compressions) and regions where it is spread out (rarefactions). Each bit of air moves back and forth along the direction of travel, and the pattern of compressions moves on: sound in air is a longitudinal wave.

## Option

Steadily away from the loudspeaker, towards the listener.

> If the air moved steadily away from the loudspeaker, music would create a wind. Each bit of air just moves back and forth about where it started. What travels to the listener is the pattern of squashed and stretched air, carrying energy.

## Option

Along a wavy path, up and down as it moves away from the loudspeaker.

> This mixes two wrong ideas: reading the wavy graph of a sound wave as a path, and thinking the air travels to the listener. Each bit of air just moves back and forth along the direction of travel, about where it started.

## Why it's tempting

The wavy line is the universal symbol for a wave. Textbook diagrams, oscilloscope screens and the waveforms in music apps all show sound as a smooth curve going up and down, and it is natural to read the curve as a picture of what the air is doing.

The first waves most people think about, waves on a rope or ripples on water, involve visible up-and-down motion. Sound seems to be another example of the same thing.

And everyday experience rarely shows the actual motion of the air in a sound wave. It is invisible, and incredibly small.

## A better picture

**Sound in air is longitudinal.** The air moves back and forth along the direction the wave travels. Where the air bunches together, the pressure is slightly higher (a compression); where it spreads out, the pressure is slightly lower (a rarefaction). The wave is a travelling pattern of these pressure changes.

**The wavy line is a graph.** Plot the air pressure against position along the sound's path, and you get a smooth curve going above and below normal pressure. The curve looks like a wave on a rope, but its up-and-down axis is pressure, not height. The graph is wavy; the air's motion is back and forth.

**The motion is tiny.** For a note at ordinary conversational loudness, each bit of air moves back and forth by only about a hundred-millionth of a metre (around 10 to 50 nanometres, depending on the pitch), far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Our ears detect astonishingly small motions.

**Why air can't do sideways.** A wave on a rope works because the rope pulls back when you shift part of it sideways. Air has no springiness against being slid sideways: slide one layer past another and nothing pulls it back, so it can't carry sideways waves. Solids can, which is why earthquakes send out both kinds of wave.

## In the classroom

- **The slinky, both ways.** Lay a slinky spring along a bench. Push and pull one end along its length to send a longitudinal pulse, then shake it sideways for a transverse one. Sound is the first kind.
- **Dots to curve.** Draw a row of dots bunching and spreading, as in a sound wave. Then plot how crowded the dots are against position. The wavy curve appears from a pattern with no up-and-down motion at all.
- **Language.** Say "the graph of the sound wave" rather than "the sound wave" when pointing at a wavy line.
- **Where it fits.** Sound in Years 7 to 10; waves in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Music apps.** The waveform shown in a music or recording app is a graph of the pressure, or the microphone signal, against time. It is not a picture of the air moving up and down.
- **Earthquakes.** Earthquakes send out two kinds of body wave: P waves, longitudinal like sound, which arrive first, and S waves, transverse, which arrive later. S waves can't travel through liquids, and the "shadow" they leave on the far side of the Earth is key evidence that the Earth's outer core is liquid.
- **Ultrasound scans.** Medical ultrasound sends longitudinal pressure waves into the body, and builds an image from their echoes.

## Going deeper

**What sets the speed of sound.** The speed of sound depends on how stiff the material is and how dense it is. Sound travels at about 340 m/s in air, about 1,500 m/s in water, and about 5,000 to 6,000 m/s in steel.

**Pressure and displacement.** In a sound wave, the pressure is highest, and also lowest, where the displacement of the air is zero, and is normal where the displacement is greatest. So the graphs of pressure and of displacement against position are a quarter of a wavelength apart.

**In solids, both kinds.** Solids resist being twisted and sheared, so they can carry transverse waves as well as longitudinal ones. A tap on a long metal rod can send both, travelling at different speeds.
