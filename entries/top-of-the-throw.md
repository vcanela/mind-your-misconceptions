## Question

You throw a ball straight up into the air. At the very top of its flight, for an instant, it is not moving at all.

Ignoring air resistance, what is the ball's acceleration at that instant?

## Option

Zero: it has stopped moving, so it isn't speeding up or slowing down.

> If the acceleration really were zero at that instant, the ball's velocity would stop changing: it would stay at zero, and the ball would hover at the top forever. Acceleration isn't about how fast something is moving, but about how fast its velocity is changing, and at the top the velocity is changing from upwards to downwards. (Nor does gravity switch off for an instant and then on again: it pulls on the ball throughout its flight.)

## Option (correct)

About 9.8 m/s² downwards, the same as at every other point of its flight.

> Right. Gravity pulls on the ball all the way up, at the top, and all the way down, so its velocity changes by 9.8 m/s downwards every second throughout. At the top, the velocity happens to be passing through zero, on its way from upwards to downwards.

## Option

More than 9.8 m/s², since its velocity is reversing direction at that instant.

> The reversal can seem like a sudden, dramatic change, needing an extra-large acceleration. But the velocity reverses smoothly, changing by 9.8 m/s every second, just as at every other point of the flight. Nothing extra happens at the top.

## Option

About 9.8 m/s² upwards, since it was moving upwards until that instant.

> This is the idea that acceleration points the way an object is moving. But on the way up, the ball is slowing down: its velocity is changing downwards, so its acceleration points downwards even while it rises. At the top, nothing about that has changed.

## Why it's tempting

In everyday speech, "accelerate" means "speed up", and acceleration is something a moving car does. A ball hanging motionless at the top of its flight seems to be doing nothing at all, so it is natural to think its acceleration is zero.

Velocity and acceleration are easily blurred together. Both describe motion, both have units involving metres and seconds, and both are taught in the same lesson. It is easy to slip into thinking that no velocity means no acceleration.

And the moment at the top really does look special. The ball seems to pause, as though for that instant gravity had let go.

## A better picture

**Velocity is how fast and which way something is moving. Acceleration is how fast its velocity is changing.** An object can have zero velocity and still be accelerating, just as a bank balance can be zero for an instant while money is flowing out of it.

**Follow the velocity.** Throw a ball up at 19.6 m/s. Each second, gravity changes its velocity by 9.8 m/s downwards:

| Time (s) | Velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 19.6 upwards |
| 1 | 9.8 upwards |
| 2 | 0 |
| 3 | 9.8 downwards |
| 4 | 19.6 downwards |

The velocity changes by the same amount every second, including through the moment it is zero. The acceleration is 9.8 m/s² downwards throughout.

**Direction is independent too.** Acceleration doesn't have to point the way an object is moving. On the way up, the ball moves upwards while accelerating downwards. A car braking moves forwards while accelerating backwards. An object going round in a circle at a steady speed accelerates towards the centre, at right angles to its motion.

## In the classroom

- **The hovering ball.** Ask what would happen if the acceleration at the top really were zero. Students quickly see that the ball would stay there forever.
- **Velocity–time graph.** Use a motion sensor or video analysis to plot the velocity of a ball thrown up and caught. While the ball is in the air, the graph is a straight line, sloping down, crossing zero at the top without any kink. The constant slope is the acceleration.
- **Draw the arrows.** Draw velocity arrows at several points on the way up and down, and the change between them. The change always points down.
- **Language.** "Deceleration" suggests a different kind of quantity; "accelerating in the direction opposite to its motion" keeps a single idea. Students will meet the word in textbooks, so if you use it, define it that way.
- **Where it fits.** Speed and acceleration in Years 9 and 10; kinematics in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Hang time.** Basketball players and dancers seem to hang in the air at the top of a jump. Near the top, they are moving slowly, so they cover little distance: in fact they spend half of their time in the air in the top quarter of the jump's height. But their acceleration never stops for a moment.
- **Juggling.** A juggler's balls seem to pause at the top of each throw, because they move slowly there, yet their velocity is changing just as fast as everywhere else.
- **A swing.** At the highest point of each swing you stop for an instant, but you don't stay there: your velocity is still changing, from forwards to backwards. The light, floating feeling at the top comes from the seat pushing up on you least there.

## Going deeper

**With air resistance.** Air resistance always acts against the motion. On the way up, it adds to gravity, so the ball slows at more than 9.8 m/s²; on the way down, it opposes gravity, so the ball speeds up at less. At the very top, the ball isn't moving, so there is no air resistance at all, and the acceleration there is just the acceleration due to gravity (less a tiny correction for the air's buoyancy).

**The pendulum.** At the end of each swing, a pendulum bob is momentarily at rest, yet for swings of moderate size that is where its acceleration is largest. In the middle of the swing, where it is moving fastest, its speed is momentarily not changing at all.

**In calculus terms.** Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, $a = dv/dt$: the slope of the velocity–time graph, not its height. A graph can pass through zero with a steep slope.
