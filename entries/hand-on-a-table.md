## Question

Atoms are often said to be "mostly empty space", because the nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom.

So why can't you push your hand through a table?

## Option

The tiny nuclei in your hand and the table collide.

> The nuclei are so tiny, and so far apart, that they would almost never come close, even if your hand could get into the table's atoms. What stops your hand happens much further out, where the atoms' electron clouds meet.

## Option

Gravity holds the table's atoms too firmly to be pushed apart.

> Gravity between atoms is unimaginably weak compared with the electric forces between their charged parts. What holds a table together, and what stops your hand, are electric forces and the behaviour of electrons, not gravity.

## Option

Your hand's atoms could slip between the table's, if you pushed hard enough.

> There are no gaps for them to slip through: in a solid, the atoms' electron clouds already meet. However hard you push, the resistance comes from the atoms themselves. Squeezing atoms into each other forces their electrons into higher-energy states, and the harder you push, the more energy that costs. Push hard enough and you would break or crush the table, not pass through it.

## Option (correct)

The atoms' electron clouds strongly resist being pushed into each other.

> Right. The "empty space" in an atom isn't empty: it is where the electrons are, spread out in clouds. Pushing two atoms into each other would force their electrons into higher-energy states, because two electrons can't share the same state (the Pauli exclusion principle). That costs a great deal of energy, so the atoms resist, and that resistance, together with electric forces, is what you feel as touching.

## Why it's tempting

The idea comes from correct physics, told a little too simply. In the famous gold-foil experiment in Rutherford's laboratory, most alpha particles passed straight through a thin gold foil, and the nucleus really is tiny: if it were a marble on the centre spot of a sports field, the atom would reach far beyond the stands, several hundred metres across.

Pictures of atoms as tiny electron dots on orbits make the rest of the atom look empty.

And "you never really touch anything" is a popular, surprising-sounding claim, shared widely online.

## A better picture

**Empty of mass: yes.** A nucleus is a few femtometres ($10^{-15}$ m) across, and an atom a few tenths of a nanometre, tens of thousands of times larger. The nucleus takes up a tiny fraction of the atom's volume, but holds more than 99.9% of its mass. As a statement about where the mass is, "mostly empty" is right.

**Empty of everything: no.** The electrons aren't small balls on orbits. Each is spread out as a cloud through the atom, the outermost ones filling it to its edge. The space between the nucleus and the edge of the atom is where the electrons are.

**Why the alpha particles got through.** Rutherford's alpha particles passed through not because nothing was there, but because electrons are far too light to deflect a heavy, fast alpha particle, just as a bowling ball rolls through a cloud of table-tennis balls. Only the nucleus, tiny, massive and carrying all the atom's positive charge, could turn one back.

**Why matter is solid.** Two electrons can't share the same state (the Pauli exclusion principle). Pushing atoms into each other would force electrons into higher-energy states, which costs a lot of energy, so the atoms resist. Pauli exclusion forces the electrons into higher-energy arrangements, and electric forces transmit the resulting push: that is what makes a table rigid and stops your hand. "Touching" *is* this resistance; there is no deeper kind of contact that we are somehow missing.

## In the classroom

- **Keep what is right.** Rutherford's conclusion about the nucleus is correct and memorable; there is no need to take it back. Just make it precise: the atom's *mass* is concentrated in the nucleus.
- **Ask the obvious question.** If atoms are empty, why can't we walk through walls? The answer leads straight to electron clouds.
- **Replace the picture.** Swap the solar-system atom for a fuzzy electron cloud with a tiny dot at its centre.
- **Where it fits.** Atomic structure in Years 9 and 10; Rutherford scattering and quantum ideas in Years 12 and 13. Chemistry teachers meet electron clouds in bonding.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"You never really touch anything."** Viral posts and videos claim that, because atoms are mostly empty, we never truly touch anything. In fact, "touching" is exactly the resistance of electron clouds to being pushed together.
- **Humanity in a sugar cube.** It is often said that if all the empty space were squeezed out of the atoms of every person on Earth, humanity would fit into a sugar cube. As arithmetic, it is roughly right: packed at the density of an atomic nucleus, 8 billion people would take up about 2 cubic centimetres. But such crushing happens only in neutron stars.
- **Rutherford's own analogy.** Rutherford described the back-scattered alpha particles as being as surprising as a shell fired at tissue paper bouncing back.

## Going deeper

**Why matter doesn't collapse.** In 1967 Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard proved that the Pauli exclusion principle is what makes ordinary matter stable. Dyson also showed that, without it, large amounts of matter held together by electric forces would collapse into ever denser states, releasing enormous energy. The stability of the matter around us is a quantum effect.

**Neutron stars.** In a neutron star, gravity is strong enough to overwhelm the resistance of electrons, crushing matter down to the density of an atomic nucleus. A teaspoon of neutron-star material would weigh billions of tonnes on Earth.
