## Question

The International Space Station orbits about 400 km above the Earth's surface.

Compare the pull of the Earth's gravity on an astronaut standing on the ground with the pull on the same astronaut aboard the space station. On the space station, the pull is about:

## Option

Zero: the station is beyond the reach of gravity.

> Gravity has no edge. It gets weaker with distance, but never stops: the Earth's gravity holds the Moon in its orbit, 384,000 km away, and the Sun's gravity holds Neptune, 4.5 billion km away. At 400 km up, the space station is barely further from the centre of the Earth than you are.

## Option

A tiny fraction: about 1% of its value on the ground.

> This is the answer of someone who knows gravity doesn't stop, but assumes it must be very weak up there for astronauts to float. Yet the station is only 400 km up, and the Earth's radius is about 6,400 km, so the station is only about 6% further from the centre of the Earth than you are. Gravity there is only a little weaker.

## Option

About half its value on the ground.

> Gravity weakens with the square of the distance from the centre of the Earth, not the height above the ground. Halving the pull would mean being about 1.4 times as far from the centre: about 2,600 km up, more than six times higher than the space station.

## Option (correct)

About 90% of its value on the ground.

> Right. The station is about 6,770 km from the centre of the Earth, compared with 6,370 km at the surface. Gravity goes as one over the distance squared, so it is $(6370/6770)^2 \approx 0.89$ of its value on the ground.

## Why it's tempting

The pictures are overwhelming: astronauts drift through the station, water forms floating spheres, pens hang in mid-air. Everything looks exactly as it would if gravity had been switched off.

Language seals it: "zero gravity", "zero-G" and "weightless" are everywhere, in news reports, films and advertising.

We also picture space as very far away. In fact the space station is closer to the ground than Auckland is to Wellington.

And the sensation we call weight isn't gravity itself. What we feel is the floor or a chair pushing up on us. Take that push away, as in a lift whose cable has snapped, or in the first moments of a drop ride, and we feel weightless while gravity is acting on us as strongly as ever.

## A better picture

**Gravity is still there.** The pull of the Earth's gravity falls off with the square of the distance from the Earth's centre. At the space station's height that distance has grown by only about 6%, so the pull is still about 89% of its value on the ground.

**Astronauts float because they are falling.** The space station, the astronauts and everything aboard are all falling towards the Earth together, at the same rate. Nothing pushes up on the astronauts, because the floor is falling just as fast as they are. With no push from the floor, they feel no weight, and relative to the station they float.

**Falling and missing.** Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain. Fire a cannonball slowly and it falls to the ground nearby; fire it faster and it lands further away. Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls: it keeps falling, and keeps missing. That is an orbit. The space station travels at about 7.7 km/s, going round the Earth roughly every 90 minutes.

**Gravity does the steering.** Without gravity, the station would fly off into space in a straight line. Gravity is what bends its path into a circle round the Earth: far from being absent, it is doing all the work.

## In the classroom

- **Calculate it.** Have students work out $(6370/6770)^2$ for themselves. The answer is usually a surprise.
- **The leaky cup.** Poke a few holes in the side of a plastic cup near the bottom, fill it with water, and let the jets stream out. Then drop the cup (outdoors, or over a bucket). While it falls, the water stops coming out: the water and cup fall together, and the water no longer presses on the sides.
- **A scale model.** If the Earth were a basketball, the space station would orbit less than a centimetre above its surface.
- **Language.** "Weightless" means nothing is pushing on you (zero apparent weight), not that there is no gravity. Prefer "in free fall".
- **Where it fits.** Gravity in Years 9 and 10; circular orbits and gravitational fields in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"Zero gravity" flights.** Aircraft that give passengers the experience of weightlessness fly a series of steep arcs. On each arc the aircraft flies the path of a freely falling object, its engines just cancelling air resistance, and everyone inside falls freely with it for around 20 to 25 seconds. Gravity is barely weaker: the plane is less than 10 km up. NASA's version earned the nickname "Vomit Comet".
- **Drop rides.** On a theme-park drop tower you feel almost weightless during the fall, for the same reason: you and your seat are falling together.
- **Headlines.** News stories regularly describe astronauts working "in zero gravity". NASA itself prefers the term "microgravity".

## Going deeper

**Einstein's happiest thought.** In 1907, Einstein realised that a person falling freely would not feel their own weight. He later called it the happiest thought of his life. It led him to the equivalence principle: inside a small, closed, freely falling room, no experiment done over a short time can tell you whether you are falling in a gravitational field or floating far out in space. (Over larger distances, the slight differences in gravity from place to place, described next, give the game away.) General relativity grew from this idea.

**Why "micro" gravity.** Inside the station, things are not perfectly weightless. The thin upper atmosphere slows the station slightly, the station turns once per orbit, and different parts of it are at slightly different distances from the Earth. Together these give steady residual accelerations of around a millionth of the acceleration due to gravity at the Earth's surface. Machinery, crew movement and thruster firings add brief jolts that can be a thousand times larger. "Micro" simply means very small.

**How far does gravity reach?** At the height of geostationary satellites, about 36,000 km up, the Earth's pull is about 2% of its value at the surface. At the distance of the Moon it is about 0.03%, small, but just what is needed to keep the Moon in its orbit.
