## Question

Some countries allow fresh fruit to be treated with gamma rays, to kill mould and bacteria, or to stop insect pests from breeding. A punnet of strawberries is exposed to gamma rays from a cobalt-60 source, and then taken out.

Are the strawberries now radioactive?

## Option

Yes, strongly: they should not be eaten.

> This is the fear that makes irradiated food controversial. But gamma rays at these energies pass through the fruit and deposit energy in it, breaking chemical bonds (which is what kills the bacteria), without changing any atomic nuclei. Nothing unstable is left behind, so once the fruit is taken away, it gives out nothing more than it did before.

## Option

Yes, slightly, but the radioactivity fades away within a few days.

> A more cautious version, in which the radioactivity is pictured fading away like the glow of a light. But no radioactivity was created in the first place. To make a nucleus unstable, radiation has to change the nucleus itself, and the gamma rays from cobalt-60 carry far too little energy to knock anything out of a nucleus.

## Option (correct)

No: they are no more radioactive than before.

> Right. The gamma rays pass through, kill microbes by breaking chemical bonds, and leave nothing radioactive behind. The strawberries are exactly as radioactive as they were before: very slightly, like all food, mainly because of naturally occurring potassium-40.

## Option

Only if they were left near the source for a long time.

> Time near the source changes how much energy the fruit absorbs, the dose, and so how many microbes are killed. But these gamma rays can't knock anything out of a nucleus, however long the exposure, so no length of time makes the fruit measurably radioactive.

## Why it's tempting

The words do a lot of damage. *Radiation* and *radioactive* sound almost the same, and it is easy to assume that anything touched by radiation becomes radioactive.

Radiation is invisible, and we associate it with danger that lingers. After nuclear accidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima, land and buildings really did stay dangerous for years, which seems to show that exposure leaves something behind.

Many people think of radiation as a kind of contagion, passed from one thing to the next. Films and cartoons, where anything near a nuclear source starts to glow, reinforce the picture.

## A better picture

**Irradiated is not the same as contaminated.** An object is *irradiated* when radiation passes through it. An object is *contaminated* when radioactive material itself (dust, liquid or gas containing unstable nuclei) gets on it or into it. The two are completely different situations.

**Irradiation.** Gamma rays and X-rays deposit energy by knocking electrons out of atoms. That can break chemical bonds, which is exactly how irradiation kills bacteria on food and medical equipment, and cancer cells in radiotherapy. But it doesn't change the nuclei, so nothing unstable is added. Once the object is taken away from the radiation, nothing more is given out, just as the walls of a room stop shining when you switch off a torch.

**Contamination.** Radioactive material keeps decaying, and keeps giving out radiation, until it is removed or has decayed away. After the Chernobyl accident in 1986, the danger came from contamination: radioactive iodine, with a half-life of about 8 days, in the first weeks, and caesium-137, with a half-life of about 30 years, for decades afterwards.

**All food is a little radioactive anyway.** Potassium is essential to life, and a small fraction of all potassium is radioactive potassium-40. A banana, for example, contains enough of it for roughly 10 to 15 nuclei to decay every second. Irradiating food doesn't change that.

## In the classroom

- **The torch.** Light a wall with a torch, then switch it off. The wall doesn't go on shining. Gamma rays are a far more energetic kind of light, and the same logic holds.
- **Two words, sorted.** Give students a set of cases (an X-ray at the dentist, sterilised syringes, the land round Chernobyl, irradiated spices) and ask them to sort each into *irradiated* or *contaminated*. Then add a patient injected with a radioactive tracer, and discuss: radioactive material is present, but on purpose.
- **Natural radioactivity.** With a Geiger counter, compare the background count with the count next to a dish of potassium chloride, sold as a low-sodium salt substitute. The potassium-40 in it gives a count clearly above background. Put the end window of the tube right at the surface of a deep dish (a few hundred grams), and count for several minutes each time.
- **Ask what changed.** For something to become radioactive, its nuclei must become unstable. Ask students whether a gamma ray passing through can do that.
- **Where it fits.** Radiation in Years 9 and 10; nuclear physics in Years 11 to 13. Biology teachers meet it with sterilisation and medical imaging.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Hospital supplies.** Many single-use medical items, such as syringes, gloves and dressings, are sterilised by gamma rays or electron beams inside their sealed packaging. Nobody worries that they are radioactive, and they aren't.
- **The Radura.** Irradiated food must be labelled as irradiated in many countries, often with the Radura, an international symbol of a plant inside a broken circle. In New Zealand the label must say so in words; the symbol is optional. Either way, it tells you the food has been irradiated, not that it is radioactive.
- **After a scan.** Someone who has had an X-ray or a CT scan is not radioactive afterwards. Someone given a radioactive tracer for a nuclear medicine scan is, for a while, because radioactive material is inside them. It is the same situation as contamination, except that it was put there on purpose, and they may be advised to keep their distance from small children and pregnant women for a day or so.

## Going deeper

**When radiation can make things radioactive.** It takes a change to the nucleus. Neutrons do this easily: nuclei absorb them, often becoming unstable isotopes. That is why the steel and concrete of a nuclear reactor become radioactive over its working life. Energetic gamma rays and X-rays can also knock a neutron out of a nucleus, but only above a threshold: about 1.7 MeV for beryllium and 2.2 MeV for deuterium, the lowest of all, and 8 MeV or more for most nuclei. Cobalt-60's gamma rays, at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, are below all of these, and the international Codex food standard limits X-rays used on food to 5 MeV and electron beams to 10 MeV, so that any induced radioactivity is negligible.

**Proton therapy.** Proton beams used in cancer treatment do create a tiny amount of short-lived radioactivity in the tissue they pass through, with half-lives from a few minutes to about twenty minutes, so almost all of it is gone within an hour or two. Some clinics even image that brief activity, with a PET scanner, to check exactly where the beam went.

**A glowing test.** Some minerals in food, such as the grains of dust on herbs and spices, store a little of the energy deposited by irradiation, and release it as a faint glow of ordinary light when heated. This thermoluminescence is used as an official test of whether spices have been irradiated. It is light from electrons, not radiation from nuclei.
