## Question

Two blocks are the same size and shape: one is made of lead, the other of wood. Each is held completely underwater, just below the surface of a tank of water.

How does the upward push of the water (the buoyant force) on the lead block compare with the push on the wooden block?

## Option

It is smaller on the lead block, which is why lead sinks.

> This is the most natural way to explain sinking: the water can't hold up the heavy lead, so it must push less. But the water's push depends only on how much water the block pushes aside, and both blocks push aside the same amount. Lead sinks because its weight is bigger than the push, not because the push is smaller.

## Option

It is bigger on the lead block, because lead is heavier.

> The water doesn't know what the block is made of. Its upward push comes from the pressure of the water on the block's surfaces, which depends on how much space the block takes up, not on what it is made of. The lead is heavier, but the push on it is the same.

## Option (correct)

It is the same on both blocks, because each pushes aside the same water.

> Right. The buoyant force equals the weight of the water a block pushes aside, and two blocks of the same size, fully underwater, push aside the same amount. For blocks of 1 litre, that is about 10 N on each. The lead block weighs about 110 N, much more than the push, so it sinks; a typical wooden block, such as pine, weighs about 5 to 6 N, less than the push, so it rises.

## Option

There is none on the lead block, because lead sinks.

> Every object surrounded by water feels an upward push from the water, whether it sinks or floats: that's why a heavy stone feels lighter when you lift it underwater. The lead sinks because its weight is bigger than the push, not because there is no push.

## Why it's tempting

In everyday life, heavy things sink and light things float, so it is natural to attribute the result to the water's push: the water "can't hold up" heavy things.

That turns a contest between two forces, the weight pulling down and the water pushing up, into a property of one force alone. The outcome, sinking or floating, gets read as the size of the push.

Floating objects add to the confusion. For anything floating at rest, the water's push really is equal to its weight, and it is easy to generalise that to everything: as though the push always matched the weight, and gave up when the object was too heavy.

## A better picture

**Where the push comes from.** Water pressure increases with depth. The bottom of a submerged block is deeper than its top, so the water pushes up on the bottom harder than it pushes down on the top. The difference is the buoyant force.

**Archimedes' principle.** The buoyant force equals the weight of the water the object pushes aside: $F_b = \rho_\text{water} V g$, where $V$ is the volume of water pushed aside. For an object that is fully underwater, it depends only on the object's volume and on the liquid, not on what the object is made of.

**Sinking or floating is a contest.** Compare the buoyant force with the object's weight. If the weight is bigger, the object sinks; if the push is bigger, it rises. A rising object comes to the surface and floats, sitting just low enough in the water to push aside its own weight of water.

**Why steel ships float.** A solid steel block sinks. Shape the same steel into a hull and it pushes aside a much larger volume of water, so the push can match its weight long before the hull is under.

## In the classroom

- **Force meter underwater.** Hang blocks of the same volume but different materials that sink (such as a set of equal-volume metal cylinders) from a force meter, in air and then fully underwater. The reading drops by the same amount for each: the push of the water is the same, even though the weights are different.
- **Plasticine ball and boat.** A ball of plasticine sinks. Shape the same plasticine into a boat and it floats. Same weight, different amount of water pushed aside.
- **Language.** Replace "the water can't hold it up" with "its weight is bigger than the buoyant force". It makes students name both forces.
- **Where it fits.** Floating and sinking in Years 7 to 10; Archimedes' principle and pressure in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers meet it with density.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Steel ships.** Huge ships made of steel, which is about eight times denser than water, float because their hulls push aside an enormous volume of water.
- **Icebergs.** Ice is a little less dense than seawater, so an iceberg floats with about nine-tenths of its volume below the surface: it sits just low enough to push aside its own weight of water.
- **Lifting underwater.** A heavy stone that is a struggle to lift on the beach is noticeably easier to lift underwater, because the water pushes up on it.

## Going deeper

**Eureka?** The story of Archimedes leaping from his bath and running through the streets shouting "Eureka!" comes from the Roman architect Vitruvius, writing about two centuries after Archimedes' death. Historians doubt it happened as told. Galileo pointed out that measuring the overflow from a crown would have been far too crude to detect a fraud, and suggested that Archimedes more likely used a balance, comparing how much weight the crown and pure gold appeared to lose when weighed in water.

**Submarines and fish.** A submarine dives by letting water into its ballast tanks, and surfaces by blowing it out with compressed air, changing its weight while its volume stays the same. Many bony fish do the reverse: they adjust the amount of gas in a swim bladder, changing their volume and so how much water they push aside, to stay neutrally buoyant at their depth.

**No buoyancy in orbit.** Buoyancy comes from water pressure increasing with depth, which happens because the water above is pulled down by gravity. Aboard the International Space Station, everything is in free fall together, the pressure doesn't increase with depth, and bubbles in a drink don't rise to the top.
