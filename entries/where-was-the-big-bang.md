## Question

Astronomers see distant galaxies moving away from us in every direction, and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away.

Where did the Big Bang take place?

## Option

At one point in space, far away, from which everything flew outwards.

> This is the picture the name suggests: an explosion at one spot, with debris flying outwards into empty space. But if we were part of the debris, away from the centre, we would see more galaxies on one side of the sky than the other, and an edge beyond which there were none. Instead, the universe looks the same in every direction.

## Option

Close to the Milky Way, since everything is moving away from us.

> It does look as though everything is fleeing from us. But an observer in any other galaxy would see exactly the same: from every galaxy, the distant ones all appear to be moving away. Seeing everything recede doesn't put us at the centre.

## Option

Nowhere: the universe has always existed, so there was no beginning.

> This was the steady-state theory, championed by Fred Hoyle and others in the mid-20th century. But the universe has changed: the cosmic microwave background, discovered in 1965, is the afterglow of a time when the whole universe was hot and dense, and the amounts of the lightest elements match what that hot early universe would have made. The steady-state theory couldn't explain either.

## Option (correct)

Everywhere: all of space was once hotter and denser, not just one spot.

> Right. The Big Bang wasn't an explosion at a point in space. It describes the whole universe being hotter and denser in the past, everywhere at once. Since then, the distances between widely separated galaxies have grown. The Big Bang happened here, and everywhere else.

## Why it's tempting

The name itself suggests an explosion. "Big Bang" was coined by the astronomer Fred Hoyle in a BBC radio broadcast in 1949. Hoyle favoured a rival theory, in which the universe had no beginning.

Animations of the Big Bang almost always show a flash of light at a single point, spreading outwards into darkness.

And the evidence really does show galaxies rushing away from us, which is exactly what debris from an explosion would do. It is natural to run the film backwards to a single spot.

## A better picture

**It happened everywhere.** The Big Bang theory says that the whole universe was hotter and denser in the past, everywhere at once. Space wasn't empty with a hot spot in the middle; the hot, dense state filled all of it.

**The evidence is evenness.** Galaxies receding faster the further away they are can't, on its own, tell an explosion from an expansion: fragments of an explosion would show the same pattern. What does tell them apart is that the universe looks the same in every direction. The cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the hot early universe, has the same temperature in every direction to about one part in 100,000, once the small effect of our own motion through it is removed. If we were off-centre in a cloud of debris, it wouldn't.

**Not expanding into anything.** What grows is the distance between widely separated galaxies. Cosmologists often describe this as space itself expanding. Nothing needs to exist outside the universe for it to expand into.

**Our view has an edge; the universe may not.** We can see only as far as light has had time to travel in the universe's 13.8 billion years. The edge of the observable universe is now about 46 billion light-years away, because space has stretched while the light was travelling. Whether the whole universe is finite or infinite is not known.

## In the classroom

- **Raisin bread.** As dough rises in the oven, every raisin moves away from every other, faster for raisins further apart, and every raisin sees itself as staying still while the others recede. Then be explicit about where the analogy fails: the loaf has a centre, an edge and an oven around it.
- **Dots on a balloon.** Draw dots on a partly inflated balloon, measure the distances between them along the surface, inflate it further and measure again. Every dot sees the others move away, faster for those further off, and no dot on the surface is the centre. The universe corresponds to the surface only.
- **Ask directly.** "Where did the Big Bang happen?" The answer, "here, and everywhere else", is the whole idea.
- **Where it fits.** The universe in Years 9 and 10; cosmology in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Animations.** Documentaries and websites almost always show the Big Bang as a bright flash at a point, spreading into black space: the very picture the theory rejects.
- **Headlines.** Phrases such as "the explosion that created the universe" are common in news stories.
- **Old television static.** A small fraction of the "snow" on an old analogue television tuned between channels came from the cosmic microwave background: the afterglow of the Big Bang, arriving from every direction.

## Going deeper

**Lemaître and Hubble.** The Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître proposed in 1927 that the universe is expanding, and in 1931 that it began in a super-dense state, which he called the "primeval atom". The idea that the early universe was also extremely hot came from George Gamow, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman in the late 1940s. In 1929 Edwin Hubble published the observations showing that more distant galaxies recede faster.

**What doesn't expand.** Atoms, people, planets, the solar system and our galaxy don't expand with the universe. They are held together by forces far stronger than the gentle effect of the expansion. The growth of distances shows up only between galaxies that are not bound to each other.

**The very beginning.** The Big Bang theory describes how the universe evolved from a very hot, dense state. It doesn't say what happened at the very beginning, if there was one: at the earliest moments, our current theories break down.
