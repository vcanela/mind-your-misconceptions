## Question

A phone battery runs from fully charged to completely flat.

Compared with when it was fully charged, what does the flat battery have less of?

## Option

Electric charge: it has used up the charge stored in its chemicals.

> This is what everyday language suggests: we talk about a phone "running out of charge" and "charging" it. But a flat battery contains the same amount of electric charge as a full one, and is electrically neutral either way. What it has lost is energy: as it discharges, it pushes charge round the circuit, and its chemicals give up energy to do so.

## Option

Electrons: they have flowed out of it into the phone.

> Electrons do flow out of one terminal, but just as many flow back in at the other: the charge goes round the circuit and isn't used up. The battery contains as many electrons when flat as when full. What has run down is the energy stored in its chemicals.

## Option (correct)

Energy: its chemicals have given up the energy they stored.

> Right. The battery's chemicals store energy. As it discharges, chemical reactions inside it push charge round the circuit, giving each coulomb energy, and the chemicals end up in a lower-energy state. The charge goes round and comes back; it is the stored energy that runs down. Recharging drives the reactions backwards, storing energy again.

## Option

Current: it has used up the current it was holding.

> A battery doesn't hold current. Current is the rate at which charge flows, and it only exists while the battery is connected in a circuit. What the battery holds is energy, stored in its chemicals.

## Why it's tempting

The word "charge" is used for both. We "charge" our phones, watch the "charge" fall from 100% to zero, and say a battery has "run out of charge". It would be strange not to think a battery is full of charge.

Battery ratings add to it. Phone batteries are rated in milliamp-hours, which is a unit of charge, and it is natural to read that as the amount of charge the battery contains.

And the picture of a battery as a fuel tank, with electricity as the fuel that flows out and gets used, fits everyday experience well, until you ask where the electricity goes.

## A better picture

**The charge is already there.** Metals are full of free electrons. When a circuit is completed, the battery pushes the electrons already in the wires round the loop. It doesn't need to supply them.

**Charge goes round, not out.** Every coulomb that leaves one terminal comes back to the other. A flat battery contains just as much charge as a full one. Inside the battery, the circuit is completed by charged atoms or molecules (ions) moving through the electrolyte; in a lithium-ion battery, lithium ions shuttle between the two electrodes.

**What the battery gives is energy.** Chemical reactions inside the battery give energy to each coulomb of charge that passes through it. The voltage measures this: a 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 joules (its EMF). As the reactants are used up, the voltage falls; when it is too low to be useful, the battery is flat.

**The milliamp-hour rating.** A battery's milliamp-hour rating tells you how much charge it can push round a circuit before it goes flat, not how much it holds. A 3,000 mAh phone battery can push about 10,800 coulombs round; at about 3.7 V, that delivers about 40,000 joules of energy.

## In the classroom

- **Follow the charge.** Ask students where the charge goes after it leaves the battery. It must come back, because it has nowhere else to go.
- **Joules per coulomb.** Define the volt as energy per unit charge from the start, so that a battery plainly supplies energy.
- **The pump.** A pump in a closed loop of pipe doesn't supply water: it pushes the water already there round the loop. Then point out where the analogy fails: cut a pipe and water pours out, but cut a wire and the current simply stops.
- **Where it fits.** Circuits in Years 7 to 10; EMF and internal resistance in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers meet the reactions inside cells.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The battery icon.** Phones show a battery "charge" percentage. It is an estimate of how much more charge the battery can push round the circuit before it goes flat, as a fraction of what it could push when full: a measure of what it can still do, not of the charge it contains, which never changes.
- **Power banks on planes.** Airline rules on spare batteries and power banks are set by their energy in watt-hours, not their charge in milliamp-hours, because energy is what matters for safety.
- **"Charging" the car.** Electric car chargers are rated in kilowatts, a rate of delivering energy, and electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours, an amount of energy.

## Going deeper

**The first battery.** In 1800 Alessandro Volta stacked discs of two different metals separated by cloth soaked in salt water, making the first electrochemical battery, the voltaic pile. It produced a steady current for the first time, transforming the study of electricity.

**Lithium-ion.** The 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino for developing the lithium-ion battery, now used in phones, laptops and electric cars.

**A heavier battery.** By $E = mc^2$, storing 40,000 joules in a phone battery increases its mass by about $4 \times 10^{-13}$ kg, less than a billionth of a gram: a real but unmeasurable change, the subject of [[flask-of-tea]].
