## Question

A sealed flask of hot tea, with no matter able to get in or out, cools down to room temperature, giving out about 60,000 joules of energy to the room.

What happens to the mass of the tea and flask?

## Option

Nothing: mass changes only in nuclear reactions.

> This is how $E = mc^2$ is usually met: in nuclear physics, alongside the mass defect. But the rule applies to any change in energy. When the flask gives out 60,000 joules, its mass falls by $E/c^2$: less than a billionth of a gram. The change is real, just far too small to detect.

## Option (correct)

It decreases, but by far too little to measure.

> Right. Whenever a system gives out energy, its mass falls by $E/c^2$. For 60,000 joules, that is about $7 \times 10^{-13}$ kg, less than a billionth of a gram: real, but far too small for any balance to detect.

## Option

It increases slightly, because cold tea is denser than hot tea.

> Cold tea is slightly denser, but that means it takes up less space, not that it has more mass: mass doesn't depend on how tightly packed something is. In fact the flask's mass decreases, by $E/c^2$, as it gives out energy: by less than a billionth of a gram.

## Option

Nothing: the same atoms are still inside, so the mass can't change.

> The atoms are the same, but the mass of a system isn't just the total of its atoms' masses at rest: it includes all its internal energy, including the energy of the atoms' jiggling. As the tea cools and gives out 60,000 joules, the system's mass falls by $E/c^2$: less than a billionth of a gram.

## Why it's tempting

Chemistry teaches conservation of mass, and every experiment a school can do confirms it. Burn magnesium in a closed container, and the mass doesn't change.

$E = mc^2$ is usually taught later, in nuclear physics, alongside the mass defect and the energy of fission. The two seem to be different rules for different kinds of reaction: one for chemistry, one for nuclei.

And the common phrase "mass is converted into energy" makes it sound like something dramatic and special, which only happens in reactors and stars.

## A better picture

**One rule for all energy.** Special relativity says that the mass of an object at rest is a measure of its total energy content: $E = mc^2$. Whenever an object gives out energy, as heat, light or anything else, its mass falls by $E/c^2$; whenever it gains energy, its mass rises.

**Chemistry changes mass too, very slightly.** Burning a kilogram of coal releases about 30 million joules. The products have less mass than the reactants by

$$\Delta m = \frac{E}{c^2} = \frac{3 \times 10^7\ \text{J}}{9 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}} \approx 3 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{kg}$$

about a third of a microgram, a few parts in ten billion of the coal's mass. No chemistry experiment could detect it, which is why conservation of mass works so well in chemistry: it is an extremely good approximation, not an exact law.

**Nuclear reactions change it more.** Fission of uranium-235 reduces the mass by about 0.1%, and fusion of hydrogen into helium by about 0.7%. Nuclei are bound far more tightly than molecules, so the energies, and the mass changes, are millions of times larger. The difference is one of scale, not of kind.

**Mass isn't really "turned into" energy.** The energy was there all along, counted as part of the object's mass. What changes is where it is: inside the object, or released to its surroundings.

## In the classroom

- **Calculate both.** Work out the mass change for burning a kilogram of coal and for fissioning a kilogram of uranium, side by side.
- **Reframe conservation of mass.** Present it as an approximation that works because chemical energies are tiny compared with $mc^2$.
- **Everyday examples.** A hot cup of tea has slightly more mass than the same tea cold; a charged battery slightly more than a flat one; a stretched spring slightly more than a relaxed one. All by amounts far too small to measure.
- **Language.** "The mass of the system decreases as energy is released" is more precise than "mass is converted into energy".
- **Where it fits.** Mass-energy equivalence in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The Sun.** The Sun radiates energy at such a rate that its mass falls by over four million tonnes every second. It is so massive that, over its whole life burning hydrogen, it will lose only a small fraction of a percent of its mass this way.
- **A power station.** A large nuclear power station producing 3 billion watts of heat loses about a kilogram of mass a year. A coal-fired power station producing the same energy loses about the same mass, spread over the millions of tonnes of coal and air it consumes.
- **Textbooks.** Many books introduce $E = mc^2$ only with nuclear reactions, and describe mass being "converted into energy", reinforcing the idea of two separate rules.

## Going deeper

**Where your mass comes from.** Most of the mass of a proton or neutron doesn't come from the masses of the quarks inside it: the masses of its three quarks add up to only about 1% of it. The rest comes from the energy of the quarks' motion and of the strong force fields binding them, which by $E = mc^2$ shows up as mass. So most of the mass of everything around you comes from energy of motion and fields inside its protons and neutrons.

**Testing E = mc² directly.** In 2005, physicists compared the tiny change in mass of atoms when they absorb a neutron with the energy of the gamma rays given out, and found that $E = mc^2$ held to better than one part in a million.
