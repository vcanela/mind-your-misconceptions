## Question

A bar magnet has its north pole at one end and its south pole at the other. You cut it in half, across the middle.

What do you get?

## Option

One piece that is a north pole, and one piece that is a south pole.

> This is the natural expectation, especially if you have just learned that positive and negative charges can be separated. But magnets don't work like that. Each half turns out to be a complete magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other: a new pair of poles appears at the cut.

## Option (correct)

Two smaller magnets, each with a north pole and a south pole.

> Right. Each half has a north and a south pole, with a new pair of poles appearing either side of the cut. Cut the pieces again, and again, and every piece is still a complete magnet.

## Option

Two pieces that are no longer magnetic: cutting destroys the magnetism.

> Cutting doesn't destroy the magnetism. A magnet's magnetism comes from countless tiny atomic magnets lined up throughout the material, and a clean cut leaves them lined up on both sides. (Heating a magnet strongly, or hammering it hard, can scramble the alignment, but cutting it cleanly does not.)

## Why it's tempting

Electric charge sets a powerful example. Positive and negative charges really can be separated, and they exist on their own. Magnets, with their two opposite poles that attract and repel, look like the same kind of thing.

School magnets encourage it too. Their two ends are often painted different colours, and the poles are described as being "at the ends", as though each end were a separate thing.

A bar magnet also seems to have a neutral middle. Iron filings cluster at the ends, and paper clips hardly stick to the centre. So the middle looks like a dividing line between a north part and a south part, ready to be cut.

## A better picture

**Every piece is a complete magnet.** Cut a bar magnet and each piece has a north pole and a south pole. The pieces get smaller and weaker, but every one has both poles.

**Where magnetism comes from.** Magnetic fields are produced by electric currents, and by the built-in magnetism of particles such as electrons. A loop of electric current makes a field with a north side and a south side. In a permanent magnet, the magnetism comes mostly from electrons, each of which behaves as a tiny magnet with both poles built in, and a magnet is a vast number of these lined up together. Every known source of magnetism, a current or a spinning electron, makes field lines that close on themselves: never a north pole without a south.

**Field lines have no ends.** Around a magnet, field lines leave the north end and curve round into the south end, then carry on through the inside of the magnet, back to the north end, as complete loops. The middle of a bar magnet only seems neutral from outside: inside the magnet, the field there is strong.

**The contrast with charge.** Electric field lines from charges start on positive charges and end on negative ones. Magnetic field lines have nowhere to start or end. That difference is the heart of it.

## In the classroom

- **Make a magnet and cut it.** Magnetise a steel sewing needle or a piece of hacksaw blade by stroking it many times in one direction with a strong magnet (a steel paper clip works, but holds its magnetism poorly). Test each end with a compass, looking for which end of the needle it pushes away: repulsion is the only proof of a pole, since a compass will attract any piece of plain steel. Snap or cut it in half (wear eye protection) and test all four ends the same way. Every piece has a north and a south.
- **Iron filings.** Sprinkle filings round a bar magnet, then round two pieces of a broken magnet held apart. The two pieces show two complete patterns.
- **Avoid fridge magnets.** Flexible fridge magnets are magnetised in narrow stripes of alternating poles across one face, so a cut piece has many poles, not one at each end. They make a confusing demonstration, though an interesting puzzle for older students.
- **Language.** Say "a magnet has two poles", rather than "the north end" and "the south end", as though the ends were separate parts.
- **Where it fits.** Magnetism in Years 7 to 10; magnetic fields and field lines in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The Earth's magnet.** The Earth behaves like a giant magnet, produced by currents in its liquid outer core. The magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole attracts the north ends of compass needles, so, magnetically, it is a south pole.
- **Magnetic strips.** The stripe on the back of many bank cards stores information as a row of tiny regions magnetised in alternating directions, each one a complete little magnet.

## Going deeper

**Dirac's argument.** No deeper principle of physics rules out a single, isolated magnetic pole, a monopole. In 1931 Paul Dirac showed that if even one monopole existed anywhere in the universe, electric charge would have to come in whole-number multiples of a basic unit. It does, which is suggestive, but it is not proof that monopoles exist.

**The search.** Many theories of particle physics predict monopoles, and physicists have hunted for them for decades, in cosmic rays, in moon rock and at particle accelerators. On 14 February 1982, Blas Cabrera at Stanford recorded a single event in his detector whose size matched the signal expected from a monopole passing through. It has never been repeated, and it is generally not accepted as a discovery.

**Monopoles that aren't.** In certain magnetic crystals called spin ices, the atomic magnets normally follow a strict local rule. Where that rule is broken, the defect behaves like an isolated north or south pole that can wander through the crystal. These are real and measurable, but they are not separate particles, and they don't break the rule below: the field lines still carry on, through the chain of atomic magnets behind them. Take the crystal away and they vanish.

**In Maxwell's equations.** The statement that magnetic field lines never begin or end is one of Maxwell's four equations: the magnetic flux out of any closed surface is always zero, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$. If monopoles were ever found, this equation would have to change.
