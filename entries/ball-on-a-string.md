## Question

You whirl a ball round on a string, in a flat circle, as seen from above. At the moment shown, the ball is at the top of the circle, moving to the left.

![A ball on a string moving in a circle, seen from above, at the top of the circle and moving to the left.](figures/ball-on-a-string.svg)

Suddenly the string breaks. Seen from above, which path does the ball follow?

## Option (correct)

![A straight path heading left.](figures/ball-on-a-string-tangent.svg)

> Right. While the string holds, it pulls the ball towards the centre, constantly turning its path into a circle. When the string breaks, nothing pulls the ball sideways any more, so it carries straight on in the direction it was moving at that instant: along the tangent to the circle. (It also starts to fall, but seen from above, its path is a straight line.)

## Option

![A straight path heading up, away from the centre.](figures/ball-on-a-string-radial.svg)

> This is what an outward "centrifugal force" would do, if there were one. But nothing pushes the ball outwards. While the string is intact, it pulls the ball inwards, and the moment the string breaks that pull stops. With no sideways force at all, the ball carries straight on in the direction it was already moving.

## Option

![A curved path bending round to the left.](figures/ball-on-a-string-curve.svg)

> This pictures the ball "remembering" its circular motion, and carrying on round in a wider curve. But a path only curves while a force pushes it sideways. With the string gone, nothing turns the ball, so its path is straight.

## Option

![A straight path heading up and to the left.](figures/ball-on-a-string-diagonal.svg)

> A compromise: the ball carries on, but is also flung outwards. Yet there is no outward push to add to its motion. With no sideways force at all, the ball keeps the velocity it had at the moment the string broke: straight along the tangent.

## Why it's tempting

The feeling is real and strong. On a spinning fairground ride you are pressed against the outside wall, and when a car takes a sharp corner you are pushed against the door. It feels exactly like a force throwing you outwards.

The word "centrifugal", meaning "fleeing the centre", is everywhere: centrifuges, centrifugal pumps, and explanations of the spin cycle of a washing machine. It sounds like the name of a real force.

And a released object really does end up further from the centre. Moving off along a straight line, its distance from the centre grows, so it looks as if it has been thrown outwards.

## A better picture

**Moving in a circle needs an inward force.** Left to itself, a moving object carries straight on (Newton's first law). To make it go round a circle, something must keep pulling it towards the centre, turning its path. For the ball, that is the string. (Strictly, a whirled string slopes slightly downwards: the horizontal part of its pull keeps the ball going round, and the vertical part holds up its weight.) That inward pull is called the centripetal force, and for an object of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ round a circle of radius $r$, it must be $F = mv^2/r$.

**Nothing pushes outwards.** There is no outward force on the ball: nothing is there to push it. Let go of the string and the inward pull stops, and the ball carries straight on, along the tangent.

**In the car.** As a car turns a corner, your body tends to carry straight on. The car turns underneath you, and the door comes round and pushes you inwards, round the corner with it. What you feel as being "thrown against the door" is the door pushing on you.

## In the classroom

- **The cut plate.** Cut a section out of the rim of a paper plate and roll a marble round the inside of the rim, fast. When it reaches the gap, it leaves along the tangent, not outwards. Students can predict the exit path first and mark it on the table.
- **Sparks from a grinder.** Show a video of a grinding wheel: the sparks fly off along the tangent at the point where the wheel meets the metal, not straight out from the centre.
- **Ask what is doing the pushing.** Whenever someone describes a centrifugal force, ask what object is exerting it. For every real force, there is an answer.
- **Where it fits.** Newton's first law in Years 9 and 10; circular motion in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The spin cycle.** In a washing machine's spin cycle, the drum pushes the clothes round a circle, but the water in them isn't held in: where it can, it carries straight on, out through the holes in the drum.
- **Bicycle wheels.** Ride through mud or a puddle and, as seen by the rider, drops fly off the tyre along tangents, which is why bicycles have mudguards.
- **The hammer throw.** An athlete whirls a heavy ball on a wire and lets go at just the right moment. The hammer flies off along the tangent, in the direction it was moving at the instant of release.

## Going deeper

**Forces that come with the viewpoint.** Physicists working in a rotating frame of reference, such as the surface of the spinning Earth, find it useful to add extra forces to make Newton's laws work: the centrifugal force, pointing outwards, and the Coriolis force, sideways to the motion. They are called fictitious, or inertial, forces, because no object exerts them; they come from the rotation of the viewpoint. They are very useful: weather forecasters use the Coriolis force to explain why large storms rotate in opposite directions in the two hemispheres.

**Artificial gravity.** A space station that spins would press its occupants against its outer wall. From inside, this would feel like gravity, with "down" pointing outwards. Seen from outside, the wall is simply pushing the occupants round a circle.

**Bathtubs and the Coriolis force.** It is often said that bathwater drains clockwise in one hemisphere and anticlockwise in the other. For a bathtub or a sink, the Coriolis force is far too weak to decide: the direction depends on the shape of the basin and any leftover swirl in the water. Only with extremely careful experiments, letting the water settle for many hours first, has the expected hemisphere effect been shown.
