## Question

In a car, you switch on the headlights, and they come on at once, a couple of metres of wire away from the battery. A current of about 5 A flows in the copper wire to each headlight.

Roughly how fast, on average, are the electrons in that wire drifting along it?

## Option

Close to the speed of light: about 300,000 km per second.

> The lights come on almost instantly, so something does travel extremely fast. But it isn't the electrons. When the switch closes, a change in the electric field races along the wires at a large fraction of the speed of light, and electrons all the way round the circuit, including those already in the headlight, start moving almost at once.

## Option

About a metre per second, roughly walking pace.

> That would mean waiting a second or two for headlights two metres away. The electrons are far slower even than that, yet the lights come on at once. They don't have to travel from the battery to the headlight: there are electrons in the headlight already, and they start moving within a few billionths of a second of the switch closing.

## Option (correct)

Less than a millimetre per second.

> Right. In a wire carrying a few amps, the electrons drift along at a fraction of a millimetre per second: drifting at that speed, an electron would take more than an hour to get from the battery to the headlight. The current is large because there are an enormous number of electrons moving together.

## Option

They don't move along at all; only energy moves.

> An understandable overcorrection. It is true that the energy reaches the headlight through the electric and magnetic fields around the wires, not by riding along on the electrons. But a current is moving charge: if the electrons didn't drift along, there would be no current, and no light.

## Why it's tempting

Electrical things respond instantly. Press the switch and the light is on, so the electricity must have got there very fast.

The phrase "electricity travels at the speed of light" is often heard, and it is nearly true of the electrical *signal*. It is natural to assume it means the electrons.

The picture of a circuit as a delivery service doesn't help: electricity sent out from the battery and arriving at the lamp, like water from a tap. That picture needs something to make the journey, and fast.

## A better picture

**Electrons drift slowly.** The average drift speed of the electrons is

$$v = \frac{I}{nAe}$$

where $I$ is the current, $A$ the cross-sectional area of the wire, $e$ the charge on an electron, and $n$ the number of free electrons per cubic metre. Copper has about $8.5 \times 10^{28}$ free electrons per cubic metre. For 5 A in a car wire with a cross-section of 1 mm², that gives about 0.4 mm per second. The current is large, even though the electrons are slow, because there are so many of them.

**The circuit is already full.** The wires and the headlight filament are full of free electrons before the switch is closed. Closing the switch changes the electric field in the circuit, and that change travels along the wires at a large fraction of the speed of light. Within a tiny fraction of a second, electrons all round the circuit, including those in the filament, start drifting together.

**A tube of marbles.** Fill a tube with marbles and push one more in at one end: one pops out of the other end at once. The push travels through the tube quickly, but no single marble travels far.

## In the classroom

- **Do the calculation.** The drift speed is one of the most surprising numbers in school physics, and it takes a single line of algebra. Then ask how long an electron would take to get from the battery to the headlight.
- **Marbles in a tube.** As above, or a line of students standing shoulder to shoulder: push the first, and the last one moves almost at once.
- **A full hose.** A garden hose already full of water squirts the moment the tap opens, but the water that comes out is not the water that has just gone in.
- **Language.** Say "the current is the same all round the circuit at once" rather than "the current flows out of the battery to the bulb".
- **Where it fits.** Current and circuits in Years 9 and 10; drift speed and $I = nAve$ in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **"At the speed of light."** Popular articles often say electricity travels at the speed of light. The signal travels at a large fraction of it; the electrons, nowhere near.
- **Network cables.** Signals in a typical network cable travel at about two-thirds of the speed of light in a vacuum, and light in an optical fibre at about the same speed. Over long distances, that delay matters: it is part of why some financial traders pay to have their computers housed as close as possible to the stock exchange's.
- **Mains electricity.** In the wiring of your house, the electrons don't travel along at all: with alternating current, they jiggle back and forth 50 times a second (60 in North America), never getting anywhere on average.

## Going deeper

**Fast, but going nowhere.** Individual electrons in copper actually move very fast, typically around a million metres per second and up to about 1.6 million, but in random directions. That speed comes from quantum mechanics, from the Pauli exclusion principle, which forces most electrons into high-energy states; ordinary thermal motion at room temperature would give about 100 km per second, more than ten times slower. The drift is a tiny overall motion superimposed on that random whizzing, like a slow breeze through a swarm of bees.

**How far does an electron jiggle?** For an alternating current of 10 A in a household wire of 1.5 mm², the electrons move only about two micrometres either side of where they started, about a fortieth of the thickness of a human hair.

**What sets the signal speed.** The change in the field travels not inside the metal, but mostly in the insulation and the space around the wires. Its speed depends mainly on the insulating material and on how the wires are arranged, which is why in everyday cables it is typically between about half the speed of light and nearly all of it. (In very long, thin wires, resistance slows it much more.)
