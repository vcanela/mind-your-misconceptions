## Question

A spacecraft far out in space fires its rocket engine.

Can this make it speed up, and if so, what does the rocket push against?

## Option

It can't speed up: with nothing to push against, it can only coast.

> Many everyday ways of moving do push against something outside: swimmers push on water, propellers on air, feet on the ground. A rocket is different: it carries the stuff it pushes against. It throws its exhaust out backwards at high speed, and the exhaust pushes back on it. Every rocket engine and thruster on a spacecraft works this way.

## Option

The thin traces of gas that are still left in space.

> The gas in space is far too thin to push against, and rockets don't need it. In fact air only gets in the way: a rocket engine gives noticeably more thrust in a vacuum than at sea level, because there is no air pressing on the rocket to partly cancel its thrust.

## Option

The pull of gravity from the nearest planet or star, which it pushes away from.

> Gravity pulls on the rocket, but it isn't something a rocket can push against. A rocket works just as well far from any planet, where gravity is negligible. What it pushes on is its own exhaust.

## Option (correct)

Its own exhaust gas, which it throws out backwards.

> Right. The engine throws hot gas out backwards at high speed. By Newton's third law, as the rocket pushes the gas backwards, the gas pushes the rocket forwards with an equal force. The rocket and its exhaust push on each other, and nothing else is needed.

## Why it's tempting

Almost every way of moving that we know pushes against something outside. We walk by pushing on the ground, swim by pushing on water, and row by pushing an oar against the water. Aircraft propellers push on air. The idea that you need something to push against is well supported by experience.

The launch itself seems to confirm it. A rocket rises on a huge plume of exhaust blasting into the ground, and it looks as if the rocket is pushing itself off the launch pad.

And in space there seems to be nothing at all, so it is hard to imagine what could give the rocket its push.

## A better picture

**The rocket and its exhaust push on each other.** A rocket engine burns fuel and throws the hot gas out of its nozzle at very high speed. To do that, the rocket pushes on the gas, backwards. By Newton's third law, the gas pushes on the rocket with an equal force, forwards. That push is the thrust.

**Think of recoil.** Stand on a skateboard and throw a heavy ball backwards: you roll forwards. You didn't push on the air or the ground: you pushed on the ball, and the ball pushed back on you. A rocket does the same thing continuously, throwing out a stream of tiny gas molecules very fast instead of one ball.

**Momentum tells the same story.** With no outside forces, the total momentum of the rocket and all its fuel, including the exhaust already thrown out, stays the same. So when gas is thrown backwards, the rocket gains an equal amount of momentum forwards.

**Air only gets in the way.** Air creates drag, slowing the rocket down. It also presses on the outside of the rocket, and because nothing balances that push across the open end of the nozzle, it partly cancels the thrust. So rocket engines give noticeably more thrust in a vacuum than at sea level, typically 10 to 25 percent more.

## In the classroom

- **Balloon rocket.** Thread a straw onto a long string stretched across the room, tape an inflated balloon to the straw, and let go. Ask what the balloon is pushing on.
- **Skateboard and ball.** A student sitting on a skateboard or a wheeled office chair, feet up, in a clear space, throws a heavy ball (no more than about 2 kg) to a catcher, and rolls the other way. Throw it harder, and they roll faster.
- **Ask the question directly.** "If rockets needed air, how did the Apollo astronauts take off from the Moon?" The Moon has almost no atmosphere: far too little to push against.
- **Language.** Say "the rocket pushes on its exhaust" rather than "the exhaust pushes on the ground".
- **Where it fits.** Newton's third law in Years 9 and 10; momentum in Years 12 and 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **The New York Times.** In 1920, a New York Times editorial mocked the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard, claiming a rocket could not work in the vacuum of space because it would have nothing to push against. On 17 July 1969, the day after Apollo 11 set off for the Moon, the paper printed a short correction: experiments had confirmed Newton's findings, a rocket can work in a vacuum, and "The Times regrets the error."
- **Recoil.** A gun kicks back when it fires, and a fire hose pushes back on the firefighters holding it. Both are the same physics as a rocket.
- **Spacewalks.** Astronauts on spacewalks move around using handholds and tethers. Some have used small gas jets, a rocket in miniature, to fly around freely.

## Going deeper

**The rocket equation.** In 1903 Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published how the speed a rocket can gain depends on its exhaust speed and how much of its mass is fuel: $\Delta v = v_e \ln(m_0/m_1)$, where $v_e$ is the exhaust speed, $m_0$ the starting mass and $m_1$ the mass once the fuel is used. Because of the logarithm, doubling a rocket's speed takes far more than double the fuel, which is why rockets are mostly fuel and are built in stages.

**Thrust in detail.** The thrust of a rocket engine has two parts: the momentum carried away by the exhaust each second, and a pressure term, which depends on the difference between the exhaust pressure at the nozzle exit and the pressure of the air outside. With no air outside, that difference is largest, which is why engines produce more thrust in a vacuum. Engines designed for space have much bigger nozzles, which would work poorly at sea level.

**Gentle but tireless.** Ion engines throw out charged atoms at enormous speed, but only a tiny trickle of them, so their thrust is very small, about the weight of a sheet of paper. Running for months and years, though, they can give a spacecraft a bigger total change in speed than any onboard chemical engine with the same amount of propellant. Dawn gained about 11 km/s under its own power, more than any spacecraft before it. NASA's Dawn mission used ion engines to travel to two bodies in the asteroid belt, Vesta and Ceres, and orbit each in turn.
