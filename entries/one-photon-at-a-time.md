## Question

In a double-slit experiment, a special source emits single photons, one at a time, with long gaps between them. A sensitive screen records the spot where each photon arrives.

After many thousands of photons have arrived, what does the screen show?

## Option

Two bright bands, one behind each slit, as for tiny particles.

> This is what tiny classical particles, like pellets, would give. Photons don't behave like that. Each photon does arrive at a single spot, like a particle, but the spots build up into interference fringes, which need something wave-like passing through both slits.

## Option

A single blurred patch, because interference needs two photons to meet.

> A natural idea: interference is two waves combining, so surely two photons are needed, and photons arriving alone would simply spread out. But the fringes appear even when photons pass through one at a time. As Paul Dirac put it, "each photon then interferes only with itself".

## Option (correct)

Interference fringes, the same pattern as with bright light.

> Right. Each photon arrives at a single spot on the screen, like a particle. But the spots aren't spread evenly: they build up, one by one, into exactly the interference fringes seen with bright light. Something wave-like must pass through both slits for each photon, yet each is detected at one place.

## Option

No clear pattern, since single photons land at random places.

> Each photon does land at an unpredictable place. But the places aren't spread evenly: photons almost never land where the dark fringes are, and most often land at the bright ones, so after many photons the fringe pattern appears.

## Why it's tempting

Classical physics offers two kinds of thing that carry energy from place to place: waves, which spread out and interfere, and particles, which are found in one place and follow definite paths. The two seem mutually exclusive, and it is natural to try to fit light into one.

The history reads like a long argument waiting to be settled. Newton favoured particles; Young's double-slit experiment in the early 1800s seemed to prove light was a wave; Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect in 1905 brought particles back.

And textbooks often present each experiment as "proof" of one side, as though light switched between being a wave and being a particle.

## A better picture

**Light is a quantum object.** Light is neither a classical wave nor a classical particle, but something our everyday categories don't fit. Both descriptions capture part of its behaviour.

**It travels like a wave.** Light spreads out, diffracts and interferes. In quantum theory, a wave-like quantity, the probability amplitude, passes through both slits and interferes, and it determines how likely the photon is to be detected at each point on the screen.

**It is detected like a particle.** When light gives up energy to matter, it does so in whole lumps, photons, each with energy $E = hf$, where $h$ is Planck's constant and $f$ the frequency. Each photon is detected at a single place.

**Asking which slit destroys the fringes.** If the apparatus is set up to record which slit each photon went through, the interference fringes disappear. Which behaviour we see depends on what the experiment measures, an idea Niels Bohr called complementarity.

## In the classroom

- **Watch it build up.** Show a time-lapse of a single-particle double-slit experiment, with spots appearing one at a time and slowly forming fringes. Such recordings exist for both photons and electrons.
- **Change the question.** Instead of "is light a wave or a particle?", ask "in this experiment, how does light behave?"
- **Two experiments side by side.** A laser through a double slit shows wave behaviour; the photoelectric effect shows particle behaviour: a negatively charged, freshly cleaned zinc plate on an electroscope is discharged by even weak ultraviolet light, but not by bright visible light.
- **Where it fits.** Wave-particle duality in Years 12 and 13 and the IB Diploma.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Grainy photos.** In very dim light, photos from a digital camera look grainy partly because the light itself arrives as separate photons: when only a few reach each pixel, the random scatter in their numbers shows up as noise.
- **Einstein's Nobel Prize.** Einstein's Nobel Prize (for 1921) cited "his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect", not relativity. Tellingly, the committee avoided endorsing his light quanta, which many physicists still doubted.
- **"Wave or particle?"** Quizzes and popular articles still pose the question as though it had a one-word answer.

## Going deeper

**Not just light.** Electrons, neutrons, atoms and even large molecules show the same behaviour. In 1989 a team at Hitachi, led by Akira Tonomura, recorded electrons building up an interference pattern one at a time, using an electron biprism, the equivalent of a double slit. In 1999 Anton Zeilinger's group in Vienna showed interference with buckyballs, molecules of 60 carbon atoms.

**Was it really one at a time?** In 1909 G. I. Taylor photographed interference with light so faint that, on average, far less than one photon at a time was in the apparatus, and the fringes still appeared. But faint light from an ordinary source doesn't truly deliver photons one by one. Experiments with genuine single-photon sources, such as one by Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Alain Aspect in 1986, confirmed both halves of the story: each photon is detected in one place, and yet single photons still interfere.
