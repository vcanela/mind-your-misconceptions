## Question

A jug holds 1 litre of water at 40 °C. A mug holds 0.2 litres of water at 90 °C.

Each is used to melt ice, until its water has cooled to 0 °C. Ignoring any energy lost to the surroundings, which one melts more ice?

## Option (correct)

The jug

> Right. How much ice melts depends on how much energy the water gives out as it cools, and that depends on the amount of water as well as on how far its temperature falls. The jug gives out about 170 thousand joules, the mug about 75 thousand: enough to melt about half a kilogram of ice, against a little under a quarter of a kilogram.

## Option

The mug

> The mug's water is hotter, so each gram of it has more energy to give out as it cools to 0 °C: a little over twice as much as a gram of the jug's water. But the jug has five times as much water, so in total it gives out more than twice as much energy: about 170 thousand joules, against 75 thousand.

## Option

They melt the same amount

> It can seem as though the jug's larger amount and the mug's higher temperature might balance out. Work out the numbers: each gram of the mug's water gives out a little over twice as much energy as a gram of the jug's, but the jug has five times as much water. The jug melts more than twice as much ice.

## Why it's tempting

Everyday language uses the two words almost interchangeably. We "turn up the heat" to raise the temperature, describe a hot day as "the heat", and ask how much heat something holds when we mean how hot it is.

Temperature is also what we feel and what we measure. A thermometer tells us how hot something is, and nothing in our experience directly shows us how much energy is stored in it. So the hotter object seems to have "more heat".

And adding energy usually does raise the temperature, which makes the two seem like one thing measured in two ways.

## A better picture

**Temperature** tells you how hot something is. It is related to the average energy of motion of its particles, and it decides which way heat flows: the net flow is always from higher temperature to lower. It doesn't depend on how much of the substance there is: a teaspoon and a bucket of the same water can be at the same temperature.

**Internal energy** is the total energy stored in all the particles. It does depend on the amount: a bucket of water at 40 °C stores far more than a teaspoon at 40 °C.

**Heat** is energy transferred from one thing to another because of a difference in temperature. Strictly, an object doesn't contain heat: it contains internal energy, and heat is energy on the move.

**Putting numbers to it.** The energy given out as water cools is $Q = mc\Delta T$, where $m$ is the mass, $c$ is the specific heat capacity (about 4.2 kJ per kg per °C for water) and $\Delta T$ is the temperature change. The mass matters just as much as the temperature change.

**Melting shows the difference starkly.** While ice melts, energy flows in and the temperature stays at 0 °C. Energy goes in; the temperature doesn't change. Heat and temperature can't be the same thing.

## In the classroom

- **Mix and predict.** Mix 100 g of water at 80 °C with 100 g at 20 °C, and ask students to predict the final temperature (about 50 °C). Then mix 100 g at 80 °C with 200 g at 20 °C (about 40 °C). The amounts matter, not just the temperatures.
- **Same temperature, different energy.** Heat a small and a large beaker of water, starting at the same temperature, on identical hotplates. Ask which reaches boiling first, and why the large one needs more energy to get there.
- **Melting plateau.** Log the temperature of crushed ice as it is warmed, stirring continuously. Ice straight from the freezer first warms to 0 °C, then stays there until it has melted. The flat stretch at 0 °C, while energy is flowing in, is the most persuasive single graph for separating heat from temperature.
- **Language.** Say "how hot" for temperature, and "energy" for the amount: "the jug stores more energy, although the mug is hotter".
- **Where it fits.** Heat and temperature in Years 7 to 10; specific heat capacity and latent heat in Years 11 to 13. Chemistry teachers meet the same distinction in calorimetry.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Sparklers.** The sparks leave a sparkler at well over a thousand degrees, yet one that lands on your hand usually stings rather than burns. Each spark is so tiny that it carries very little energy, and it cools almost at once. (The wire is another matter: it holds far more energy and stays hot enough to burn.)
- **Oven foil.** You can whip a sheet of aluminium foil off a hot oven dish with your fingers, but you would never grab the dish. Foil and dish are at the same temperature; the thin foil holds very little energy.
- **"Turn up the heat."** The everyday phrase for raising the temperature is a small, daily reinforcement of the misconception.

## Going deeper

**The million-degree corona.** The Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, has a temperature of over a million degrees. NASA's Parker Solar Probe flies through it, protected by a heat shield built to withstand about 1,400 °C; on its closest passes the shield is expected to reach about 1,000 °C. The spacecraft gets nowhere near the corona's temperature because the corona is so thin: its particles are extremely hot, but there are so few of them that they deliver little energy. Most of the heating comes from direct sunlight, which the shield blocks, keeping the electronics behind it at about 30 °C.

**Temperature and particle energy.** For a gas, temperature measures the average translational kinetic energy of the particles, their energy of moving from place to place: $\tfrac{3}{2}kT = \tfrac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle$, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant. Double the number of particles at the same temperature, and you double the energy, but not the temperature.

**Heat as a verb.** Physicists treat heat, like work, as a process: a way of transferring energy, not something an object has. The older picture of heat as a substance, the 18th-century "caloric", lives on in our language, in phrases like "heat content" and "the heat in the room".
