## Question

Out at sea, in deep water far from the shore, a cork floats on the surface. A series of waves passes beneath it, heading towards a distant beach.

What does the cork do?

## Option

It travels towards the beach with the waves, at the speed of the waves.

> The waves certainly travel towards the beach, so it seems the water and the cork must go too. But what travels is the shape of the wave, not the water. Each bit of water moves round in a small loop and comes back almost to where it started, and the cork does the same.

## Option

Each crest pushes it a little towards the beach, and it waits there for the next crest.

> This pictures the wave as a series of shoves. In fact the cork moves backwards in each trough almost as far as it moved forwards on the crest before, so over a whole wave it goes almost nowhere.

## Option

It bobs straight up and down, ending each wave about where it started.

> Close, and much nearer the truth than being swept along. But water waves move the surface backwards and forwards as well as up and down, so the cork traces a loop rather than a straight line. In deep water the loop is a circle.

## Option (correct)

It goes round in small loops, ending each wave about where it started.

> Right. In deep water, each bit of the surface moves in a small circle as a wave passes: forwards on the crest, backwards in the trough. The cork rides these circles and ends each wave almost where it began. (Strictly, the loops don't quite close, so the cork creeps forward very slowly.)

## Why it's tempting

The wave so obviously moves. Watch the sea and you see crests marching towards the shore, and it is natural to assume that the water is marching too.

The beach seems to confirm it. Surfers ride waves in to shore, and seaweed and driftwood wash up on the sand. But that happens in shallow water, where waves steepen and break and the water really is thrown forwards, and where wind and currents also play a part. Out in deep water, the motion is quite different.

Sound encourages the same idea: something seems to travel from the loudspeaker to your ear, so surely something material must make the journey.

## A better picture

**A wave is a travelling disturbance.** The material it travels through, water, air, a rope or a spring, moves back and forth about where it started. What travels is the pattern of the disturbance.

**Water waves.** In deep water, each bit of the surface moves in a circle as the wave passes, and the circles get smaller with depth: half a wavelength down, they are only about 4% of their size at the surface. The water at any one place goes round and round; only the shape of the wave moves on.

**Sound waves.** In a sound wave, the air moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels, making a pattern of squashed and stretched regions that moves away from the source. No air is carried from the loudspeaker to your ear: the air near the speaker just moves back and forth. (Very loud sound can stir up slight steady draughts, called acoustic streaming, but that is a small side effect, not how sound travels.)

**What waves do carry.** Waves carry energy from one place to another, which is how the sea wears away cliffs and how sound vibrates your eardrum. They also carry information, and they can push on whatever absorbs or reflects them.

## In the classroom

- **A marked spring.** Tie a ribbon to one coil of a stretched slinky spring and send a pulse along it. The pulse travels the length of the spring; the ribbon moves and returns to where it was.
- **The cork.** Float a small, light float (a cork offcut or a polystyrene bead, much smaller than the wavelength) in a long trough with a sloping "beach" or a sponge at the far end to stop reflections. Make gentle, regular waves at one end: the float moves round in small loops and stays almost where it was.
- **A Mexican wave.** Line the class up and pass a wave along by standing and sitting in turn. The wave travels; nobody moves along.
- **Waves and currents.** Point out that currents, such as rivers and ocean currents like the Gulf Stream, do move water from place to place. They are bulk flows, not waves, and the two often happen together. (The tide is a special case: it is itself a huge, slow wave, but its wavelength is so long that tidal currents can carry water several kilometres before they turn.)
- **Where it fits.** Waves in Years 9 and 10; wave motion in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Stadiums.** A Mexican wave runs round a stadium at about twenty seats per second, while every spectator stays in their own seat.
- **Wheat in the wind.** On a breezy day, as gusts sweep over a field of wheat, waves of bending stalks race across it, yet every stalk stays rooted where it is.
- **Tsunamis.** In the open ocean, a tsunami is often less than a metre high and passes unnoticed under ships, while travelling at the speed of a jet aircraft, about 700 km/h in water 4 km deep. The water itself moves only a few centimetres per second, swaying a few metres back and forth from the surface to the sea floor; the energy races on. Only near the coast, where the wave slows and grows, does the water surge inland.

## Going deeper

**Stokes drift.** The circles traced by the water in a wave don't quite close: the water moves a little further forwards on each crest than it moves back in each trough. The result is a slow net drift in the direction of the waves, called Stokes drift, after George Stokes, who described it in 1847. It is small compared with the wave's speed, but it matters for the way floating debris, oil spills and plastics spread across the oceans.

**Breaking waves.** As a wave moves into shallow water, the bottom gets in the way of the circular motion. The circles flatten into ellipses, the wave slows and steepens, and eventually the crest topples forwards. At that point the water really is thrown towards the beach, and the simple picture no longer applies.

**Light carries momentum.** As well as energy, light carries momentum, so it pushes on whatever absorbs or reflects it. The push is tiny, but in space it can be used: in 2010 the Japanese spacecraft IKAROS unfurled a thin sail and became the first spacecraft to demonstrate a solar sail in interplanetary space, steering by the pressure of sunlight.
