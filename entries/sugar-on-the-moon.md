## Question

An astronaut takes a 1 kg bag of sugar to the Moon, where gravity is about one-sixth as strong as on Earth.

On the Moon, which of these is different from what it was on Earth?

## Option

Its mass

> Mass is the amount of matter in the bag, and it doesn't change from place to place: the bag holds exactly the same sugar on the Moon. What changes is its weight, the pull of the Moon's gravity on it.

## Option (correct)

Its weight

> Right. The bag's mass, the amount of sugar, is still 1 kg, and it is just as hard to shake or to set moving. Its weight, the pull of gravity on it, drops from about 9.8 N on Earth to about 1.6 N on the Moon.

## Option

Both its mass and its weight

> The weight changes, but the mass doesn't. The bag holds the same sugar, and it is just as hard to shake or to start moving as on Earth. On the Moon it is still 1 kg of sugar; it is simply pulled down about one-sixth as hard.

## Option

Neither: a kilogram is a kilogram anywhere.

> Half right: the mass is still 1 kg. But its weight, the force of gravity pulling it down, is about one-sixth of its Earth value: about 1.6 N instead of 9.8 N.

## Why it's tempting

Everyday language merges the two. We "weigh" ourselves and get an answer in kilograms, buy fruit "by weight" priced per kilogram, and say a heavy object "weighs a lot" whether we mean its mass or the effort of lifting it.

On Earth, the two always go together. Twice the mass always means twice the weight, so there has never been any need to tell them apart.

Volume gets drawn in too. Bigger things are usually heavier, so "how big", "how heavy" and "how much" blur into one idea of "amount", until a big cardboard box turns out to be light and a small lead weight turns out to be heavy.

## A better picture

| Quantity | What it is | Unit | Changes on the Moon? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass | The amount of matter; how hard it is to speed up | kilogram (kg) | No |
| Weight | The pull of gravity on it, $W = mg$ | newton (N) | Yes |
| Volume | The space it takes up | cubic metre (m³) or litre | No |

**Mass** is the amount of matter in something, and it also measures how hard the object is to speed up, slow down or shake. It is the same everywhere in the universe.

**Weight** is a force: the pull of gravity on the object. It depends on the mass and on the strength of gravity where the object is. On Earth, $g$ is about 9.8 N per kg; on the Moon it is about 1.6 N per kg.

**Volume** is the space the object takes up. Density, mass divided by volume, links mass and volume, but they are still different quantities: a kilogram of feathers and a kilogram of lead have the same mass and very different volumes.

## In the classroom

- **Balance or spring?** A beam balance (not an electronic one) compares an object with standard masses, and would give the same reading on the Moon, because the weaker gravity reduces the pull on both sides by the same factor. A spring scale, or an electronic balance, measures a force, and on the Moon would read about one-sixth as much.
- **The shake test.** Put a heavy object and a light one in two identical bags and shake them from side to side. The one with more mass is harder to shake back and forth. That is mass at work, and it would be just as hard to shake on the Moon, or in orbit.
- **Same mass, different size.** Show equal-mass blocks of different materials, and equal-size blocks of different masses.
- **Language.** Use kilograms for mass and newtons for weight, consistently, even though shops and bathroom scales don't.
- **Where it fits.** Mass, weight and density in Years 7 to 10; gravitational fields in Years 11 to 13.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Bathroom scales.** Bathroom scales measure the force you press down with, and display it in kilograms by assuming Earth's gravity. On the Moon, the same scales would tell you that you had lost five-sixths of your mass.
- **Weighing astronauts.** Astronauts in orbit can't use ordinary scales, because they are in free fall. Instead, they measure their mass using inertia: one device pushes them with a known spring force and measures how quickly they accelerate; others time how fast a spring-mounted seat bounces back and forth.
- **Moonwalking.** On the Moon, the Apollo astronauts' spacesuits and backpacks felt light to carry, but had their full mass. Starting, stopping and turning needed just as much sideways force as on Earth, but with their weight cut to a sixth, their boots' grip on the ground could supply only a fraction of it, so they had to lean into starts and plan their stops. Their stiff, pressurised suits made a bounding hop easier than walking.

## Going deeper

**Two kinds of mass.** Mass appears in physics in two roles: as what gravity pulls on, and as resistance to being accelerated. Experiments show these are in the same proportion for every material, to extraordinary precision: the subject of [[bowling-ball-and-tennis-ball]].

**Defining the kilogram.** From 1889 to 2019, the kilogram was defined as the mass of a particular cylinder of platinum and iridium kept in a vault near Paris. Since 20 May 2019, it has been defined instead by fixing the value of Planck's constant, so that it no longer depends on any one object.

**Gravity varies on Earth too.** The strength of gravity varies slightly over the Earth's surface, from about 9.78 N per kg at the equator to about 9.83 N per kg at the poles, because of the Earth's spin and its slightly squashed shape. A sensitive spring scale, or an electronic balance, would show a slightly different reading for the same bag of sugar in different places, which is why electronic balances are calibrated where they are used; a beam balance would not.
