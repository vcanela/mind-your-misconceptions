## Question

Which of these gives a person the biggest dose of ionising radiation?

## Option (correct)

A return flight between Auckland and London

> Right. At cruising altitude there is much less air above you to block cosmic rays, so a return flight between Auckland and London, around 50 hours in the air, gives roughly 0.1 to 0.2 mSv. That is more than all the others put together, though still only a small fraction of a year's natural background radiation.

## Option

A dental X-ray

> A dental X-ray gives about 0.005 mSv, roughly the same as a single day of natural background radiation. The long return flight gives twenty or more times as much, from cosmic rays at cruising altitude.

## Option

A year living next to a normally operating nuclear power station

> Many people expect this to be the biggest. In fact, a nuclear power station operating normally adds less than 0.01 mSv a year to the dose of people living next to it: less than one percent of natural background. The long return flight gives ten or more times as much.

## Option

Eating a banana

> Bananas contain radioactive potassium-40, so eating one does give a dose, but a tiny one, about 0.0001 mSv (and since the body soon excretes the extra potassium, even that is generous). The return flight gives about a thousand or more times as much.

## Why it's tempting

"Radiation" is one word for many things. It covers light, radio waves and heat, as well as the radiation from radioactive materials, and in everyday use its frightening meaning tends to spread to all the others.

Nuclear accidents, and the long shadow of nuclear weapons, make "nuclear" feel like the most dangerous source of all, while things that feel natural, such as flying, eating or the ground beneath us, feel harmless.

And the doses themselves are invisible and unfamiliar. Without numbers, there is no way to compare them, and fear fills the gap.

## A better picture

**Two kinds of radiation.** *Non-ionising* radiation, such as radio waves, microwaves, infrared and visible light, doesn't carry enough energy per photon to knock electrons out of atoms. *Ionising* radiation, such as X-rays, gamma rays, alpha and beta particles, and the most energetic ultraviolet, does, and can damage DNA. Non-ionising doesn't mean harmless: ultraviolet from the Sun causes sunburn and skin cancer, and intense microwaves can burn by heating. But the mechanisms are different.

**For ionising radiation, the dose matters.** Doses are measured in sieverts (Sv), usually in millisieverts (mSv). Everyone receives a natural background dose, of about 2 mSv a year in New Zealand, from radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays and the potassium-40 in our own bodies.

| Source | Typical dose (mSv) |
|---|---|
| Eating a banana | 0.0001 |
| Dental X-ray | 0.005 |
| A year living next to a nuclear power station | less than 0.01 |
| Chest X-ray (single view) | about 0.02 |
| Return flight, Auckland to London | about 0.1 to 0.2 |
| A year of natural background (New Zealand) | about 2 |
| CT scan of the chest | several |

**Context, not fear.** The risk from a dose of ionising radiation is generally assumed to be proportional to the dose, with no completely safe threshold. At the doses in everyday life, the risk is very small, and usually far outweighed by the benefits, as with medical scans.

## In the classroom

- **Rank the doses.** Give students cards with the sources in the table and ask them to put them in order before revealing the numbers. The positions of the flight and the nuclear power station are usually the big surprises.
- **Measure the background.** A Geiger counter left running in the classroom shows that radiation is around us all the time.
- **Sort the spectrum.** Ask students to sort radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays into ionising and non-ionising.
- **Language.** Use "ionising radiation" when that is what you mean, rather than "radiation" alone.
- **Where it fits.** Radiation and the electromagnetic spectrum in Years 9 and 10; dose and radioactivity in Years 11 to 13. Biology teachers meet it with mutation and medical imaging.

## Spotted in the wild

- **Phones and Wi-Fi.** Worries about "radiation" from phones and Wi-Fi routers concern non-ionising radio waves, which can't break chemical bonds the way ionising radiation can.
- **Aircrew.** Airline pilots and cabin crew receive more radiation at work than most nuclear power station workers, because they spend so long at altitude. In the European Union, airlines must assess the doses of crew likely to receive more than 1 mSv a year (usually by calculation from their routes), take them into account when planning rosters, and tell crew their doses.
- **Radon.** Radon gas seeping up from the ground is, on average, the largest single source of natural radiation worldwide. In some granite regions, such as Cornwall in England, it gives several times the usual dose, so some countries test homes and fit ventilation to reduce it.

## Going deeper

**What a sievert measures.** The sievert accounts for both the energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue and how damaging the particular kind of radiation is. For a whole-body figure such as those in the table (the effective dose), it also allows for how sensitive the exposed organs are. Alpha particles, for example, do far more damage for the same energy than gamma rays, so they count more.

**The linear no-threshold model.** Radiation protection assumes that risk rises in proportion to dose, all the way down to zero. Above about 100 mSv, studies such as those of the atomic-bomb survivors show cancer risk rising roughly in proportion to dose. At the very low doses of everyday life, the risk is too small to measure directly, and scientists still debate whether the straight-line assumption over- or underestimates it. It is used because it is cautious.

**High natural backgrounds.** In a few places in the world, such as parts of Ramsar in Iran and Kerala in India, natural background doses are many times the world average, because of radioactive minerals in the ground. Studies of the people living there have not found clear evidence of increased cancer rates, although the studies are limited.
